# GENERATIVE AI
**Generative models** are a class of machine learning models that can **generate new data** that is **similar** to what they **were trained on**. 

These models are trained to **understand** the **underlying patterns** and **structure** of the data.
There are many types of generative models, such as **generative adversarial networks** (**GANs**, discussed in this chapter), *variational autoencoders* (**VAEs**), **diffusion models** (discussed further down this chapter), and *normalizing flow models*. 
These models can be used to generate a **wide variety** of data, including **images**, **text**, and **audio**.

To understand **how clever** the concepts behind these models are, consider what Yann LeCun said about *generative adversarial networks*:

> *"[They are] the **most interesting idea** in the last 10 years in Machine Learning"*

Obviously, such a compliment coming from a prominent researcher in the area is always great advertisement and, indeed, GANs have had a huge success since their introduction in 2014 by **Ian J. Goodfellow** and his co-authors.

The ability to generate new content might make generative models look a little bit like "magic" at first sight, but hopefully the following paragraphs will help overcome this first impression and give helpful insights on the fundamentals behind these models.

>[!warning] GENERATIVE AI, DEEPFAKES AND ETHICS
>The use of generative AI to create **deepfakes** has **increased significantly** since the release of these models.
>
>Deepfakes can be used for entertainment, but also to **spread misinformation**, create **fake news**, or **manipulate public perception**, posing significant **ethical** and **security risks**.
>
>Because the capabilities of these models allows for the **rapid** and **widespread** dissemination of **misinformation**, it is becoming **increasingly challenging** for individuals to **indentify false narratives**.
>Many people are **growing skeptical** of the authenticity of the information they encounter, leading to a **fragmented** and **polarised society**. 
# GENERATIVE ADVERSARIAL NETWORK
Let us now dive into the architecture and ideas that make GANs possible.

>[!def] GENERATIVE ADVERSARIAL NETWORK
>A **generative adversarial network** is composed of **two parts**:
>- A **generator**, which learns to **generate plausible data**. The generated instances become *negative examples* for the *other part*.
>- A **discriminator**, which learns to **distinguish** the generator's **fake data** from **real data**. The discriminator **penalises the generator** for **producing implausible results**.

Thus, a GAN can be schematised as shown below:
![[22_01_GANScheme.png]]

Both the *generator* and the *discriminator* are **neural networks**.
The **generator output** is **connected directly** to the **discriminator input**. Through *backpropagation*, the **discriminator's classification** provides a **signal** that the **generator** uses to **update its weights**.

>[!note] NOTES
>1. In most cases, the **discriminator** only serves as a means to **optimise the generator**, which is the **network we are interested in training**. Thus, the *discriminator* is *usually ignored after training ends*.
>2. The **generator** and **discriminator** networks are **often CNNs** (altough some other architectures may also be used).
## REFRAMING THE PROBLEM
Now that we have discussed the *general idea* behind GANs, let us dive into **how exactly they work**.

Suppose that we are interested in **generating** black and white square **images of dogs** of size $n\times n$ pixels.
We can **reshape** each image as an $N=n^2$ dimensional **vector** (e.g. by concatenating rows/colums one after another).

Clearly, not all vectors represent a dog once shaped back to a square matrix: we can **hypothesise** that the $N$-dimensional **vectors** that effectively **encode** images of **dogs** are **distributed according** to some **very specific probability distribution** over the $N$-dimensional **vector space**.

>[!idea] GENERALISING THE HYPOTHESIS
>It thus seems reasonable to hypothesise that, in general, there **exist probability distribution functions** for **images** of **all other classes** of objects (e.g. cats, birds, etc.) over this **vector space**.

Then, the problem of **generating new images of dogs** is **equivalent** to the problem of **generating vectors** that **follow the "dog probability distribution"** (at least in our example case, the class will of course differ in each application).

The **tricky part** is that, obviously, we **do not know** how to explicitly **express this distribution**.
## INPUT SEED AND DIRECT MATCHING
To understand **how to achieve** the **desired** output **distribution**, it is important to **first think about what kind of input our network expects**.

*Normally*, a network's input is *data* that we *want to do something with*, like an instance that we want to *classify* or *make a prediction* about. 
But what do we use as input for a network that **outputs entirely new data instances?**

>[!note] RANDOM NOISE INPUT
>In its most **basic form**, a GAN takes **random noise** as its **input**. 
>The **generator then transforms** this noise into a **meaningful output**.

Therefore, our generator essentially needs to **learn a transformation** that maps a **source of noise** to **structured data**. 

>[!idea] INTERPRETING THE INPUT
>We can think of the input as a **seed** that the network uses to **produce its output**.
>Because this seed is **noisy**, we can get the GAN to produce a **wide variety of data**: we are essentially using noise to **sample** from **different parts** of the **target distribution**. 

The figure below illustrates this concept:

![[22_02_SeedToOutput.png]]

Experiments suggest that the **distribution of the noise doesn't matter much**, so we can choose one that is easy to sample from, like a **uniform distribution**. 
Furthermore, for convenience, the space from which the **noise is sampled** is often of **smaller dimension** than the dimensionality of the **output space**.

Before diving into how to optimise the generator with the help of a discriminator, it may be useful to first consider a **simpler approach**: **Generative Matching Network** (**GMNs**).
The idea behind these models is to **train them** by **comparing** the **generated distribution** to the **true one**.

>[!note] COMPARING THE DISTRIBUTIONS
>As already stated above, we **do not know** how to explicitly express the **target distribution**, and the **generated distribution** might too be **excessively complex**.
>
>However, methods to **compare distributions based on samples** exist, and these can indeed be used, as both *true data* and *generated data* samples are available. 

Therefore, we can train a GMN by repeating the following steps:

>[!tldr] TRAINING A GMN
>1. Generate some **uniform inputs**.
>2. Perform **forward propagation** through the network to obtain outputs.
>3. **Compare** the **true distribution** and the **generated distribution** based on the available **samples** to calculate **loss**.
>4. Use **backpropagation** to compute the gradients and **update** the model's **parameters**.

Notice that this is the same process we have already discussed for other networks in the previous sections; the only difference stems from the fact that the **"loss function"** here is a **measure of distance between two probability distributions** (e.g. Maximum Mean Discrepancy).

![[22_03_DirectMatching.png]]

>[!note] DIRECT MATCHING
>As shown in the figure above, at the **end of the training phase** (assuming a perfect process) the **generated distribution matches the true one**; this approach is thus also known as **direct matching**. 
## INDIRECT TRAINING
The **direct approach** presented above **compares directly** the *generated distribution* to the *target distributiong* during **training** using methods that **measure the distance** between the **two distributions**.
This introduces a problem, related to the **curse of dimensionality**.

>[!note] ISSUE WITH DISTANCE MEASURES
>When working with **high-dimensional spaces**, methods that measure the distance between probability distributions **fail to be accurate**.
>Intuitively, this is because the **volume of the space grows so fast** (and thus so do distances) that **distance fails** to **capture meaningful information**.

This issue can be addressed by **replacing** the **direct matching** process with an **indirect approach**, with the supervision of a **discriminator**.
During training, the **discriminator "judges"** the **generator's output**, forcing it to **produce more plausible outputs** that will in turn more easily **fool the discriminator**.

At the end, this process will too lead the **generated distribution** to **match the target** one, just like in the direct matching approach.
To convice ourselves of this equivalence of the two approaches, consider the figure below:

![[22_04_MatchingWithDiscriminator.png]]

In the figure above, we can see the **target distribution** in *blue*, the **generated distribution** in *orange*, and the **probability** that the **discriminator correctly distinguishes fake and real data** (computed as if it were to choose the distribution with higher density) in *grey*.

>[!idea] "FOOLING" THE DISCRIMINATOR
>We can see that the **discriminator's output** is **more likely** to be **correct** when the two **distributions** take **different values**.
>Similarly, it becomes **less confident** when the **distributions match** (around $0$ in the top situation, and over the entire range of values in the bottom case).
## SOME MATHEMATICAL DETAILS
**Mathamatically**, GANs can be modelled with a **game theoretic approach** as follows.

To start, we define the **noise source** distribution as $p_{z}(z)$ (as stated above, this is often **uniform**) and the **real data source** as $p_{r}(x)$.
To learn the **generator's distribution** $p_{g}$ over **data** $x$, we represent the **mapping** to the **data space** as $G(z;\theta_{g})$, where $G$ is a differentiable function represented by a **multilayer perceptron** with **parameters** $\theta_{g}$.
We also define a **second MLP** $D(x;\theta_{d})$ that **outputs** a **single scalar** $D(x)$ which represents the **probability** that $x$ came from the **real data** rather than $p_{g}$ (that is, the probability that $x$ is *"genuine"*).
With the given definitions, the **training process** of a GAN can be formulated as a **minimax game**:

>[!def] GAN GAME
>$$
>	\underset{ G }{ \text{min } }\underset{ D }{ \text{max } } V(D,G) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{r}(x)} [\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{z}(z)} [\log(1 - D(G(z)))]
>$$

Notice that the above **loss function** derives from **Binary Cross-Entropy Loss**, though the sign is reversed for the sake of simplicity.

>[!idea] OBSERVATIONS
>1. The **first term** deals with inputs sampled from the **real data**, while the second term accounts for the **generated data** fed to the discriminator.
>2. Notice that if $G$ generates a **perfect fake** we will have $D(G(z))\approx1$, and thus $\log(1-D(G(z)))\to-\infty$. As a result, we want to **minimise** $V$ with respect to the **generator** (because making it *very negative* means it successfully *fooled the discriminator*). Conversely, we want to **maximise** $V$ with respect to the **discriminator**: in fact, the two terms take their maximum values ($0$) when the **data is real** ($\log D(x)=\log(1)=0$) and when **a fake** input is **correctly spotted** ($\log(1-D(G(z)))=\log(1-0)=0$).

Now suppose that, for a **fixed generator** $G$, we want to find the **optimal discriminator** $D$; that is, we want to find:
$$
	\underset{ D }{ \text{max } } V(D,G) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{r}(x)} [\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{z}(z)} [\log(1 - D(G(z)))]
$$
The second expectation is *over noise drawn* from $p_{z}$ and $G(z)$ ultimately produces a **fake sample** according to $p_{g}$. But $p_{r}$ and $p_{g}$ have the **same support** $\mathcal{X}$, therefore we can rewrite the second expectation as:
$$
	\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{z}(z)} [\log(1 - D(G(z)))] = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{g}}[\log(1-D(x))]
$$
Since both distributions are continuous, we can rewrite $V(D,G)$ as follows:
$$
	V(D,G) = \int_{\mathcal{X}} p_{r}(x) \log D(x) + p_{g}(x) \log(1-D(x))dx
$$
Now we want to **maximise this integral**. Because the are no constraints linking $D(x_{1})$ with $D(x_{2})$ for any $x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}$, we can **equivalently maximise the integrand**.
For simplicity, we define a *helper function* $f(D)$ for a *fixed* $x$:
$$
	f(D) = a\log(D) + b\log(1-D)
$$
Notice that we have $a=p_{r}(x)$ and $b=p_{g}(x)$, both *constants* for a *specific value* of $x$.
To maximise $D$, we take its derivative and set it to zero:
$$
	\frac{df(D)}{dD} = \frac{a}{D} - \frac{b}{1-D}
$$
$$
	\frac{df(D)}{dD} = 0 \implies D^* = \frac{a}{a+b}
$$
And we obtain $D^*$ by substituting $a$ and $b$:
$$
	D^*(x) = \frac{p_{r}(x)}{p_{r}(x) + p_{g}(x)}
$$
This is indeed a **maximum**, as $f''(D)<0$ for all $x$.

>[!idea] IMPLICATION
>We immediately notice that, if $p_{g}(\hat{x})=p_{r}(\hat{x})$ for some $\hat{x}\in\mathcal{X}$ (that is, a specific image is **equally likely** to appear in the **target distribution AND** in the **generated distribution**), then $D^*(\hat{x})=0.5$: the **discriminator** is **completely uncertain** about the origin of $\hat{x}$.
>
>Consequently, if $p_{g}(x)=p_{r}(x)\text{ }\text{ }\forall x\in\mathcal{X}$ it means we have managed to build the **perfect generator**.

Notice that we substantially built a solid **mathematical basis** for the **intuitions** that we had seen earlier when discussing **indirect training**. 
# DIFFUSION MODELS
Another **approach** to **generative AI** is given by **diffusion models**, which are highly employed to generate **high quality images and videos** (a famous diffusion model is Midjourney).

**Both** GANs and diffusion models **start with a prior** (*noise*) and **learn a mapping** to some **complex data space**.
However, they differ in how the way they tackle the problem: unlike GANs, which required the "*supervision*" of a discriminator; diffusion models **break the transformation** into a **multitude of tiny, incremental steps** (hence the name, which resembles natural diffusion processes in physics).

>[!idea] INTUITION
>The intuition behind this approach is that the model can **correct itself** over these **small steps** and **gradually produce** a good sample.

In particular, diffusion models work in a **dual-phase mechanism**:
1. First, we **introduce noise into the dataset**.
2. Then, we **train a network** to methodically **reverse this process**.

>[!note] NOTE
>Before beginning the diffusion process, data needs to be properly adjusted for the training phase.
>This involves **removing outliers**, **data normalisation** to *scale features consistently*, and **data augmentation** to *increase dataset diversity* (see > [[1.1 - MORE ON NETWORK TRAINING#DATA AUGMENTATION]]).
>Furthermore, **different data types** may require **specific preprocessing steps**.
## FORWARD DIFFUSION
The goal of the first phase is to **add noise** to the **dataset**: this is done with a process known as **forward diffusion**.

Mathematically, given a **starting data-point** $\mathbf{x}_{0}$ sampled from the **real data** distribution $q(\mathbf{x})$ (that is, $\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim q(\mathbf{x})$), we define a **forward diffusion process** of $T$ **steps** as follows:
$$
	q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t};\sqrt{ 1-\beta_{t} }\mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \beta_{t}I) \text{ , }t=1,\dots,T
$$
Where each $q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})$ is a **multivariate distribution** with **means** given by the vector $\mu_{t}=\sqrt{ 1-\beta_{t} }\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ and **covariance matrix** $\Sigma_{t}=\beta_{t}I$ (notice that this matrix is diagonal: all **variables** are **independent**).

>[!note] CLARIFICATION
>Here, the **data points** $\mathbf{x}$ and their **distribution** $q(\mathbf{x})$ are treated as **vectors**.
>Furthermore, though diffusion models can be used to generate various kinds of data, we will present them in the context of **image generation** for the sake of clarity.

The complete forward process can be expressed as follows:
$$
	q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{0}) = \prod_{t=1}^T q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})
$$
>[!idea] OBSERVATION
>At **each step**, we are producing a **new latent variable** $\mathbf{x}_{t}$ with distribution $q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})$ by **adding Gaussian noise** with **variance** $\beta _t$ to the **previous variable** $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$.
>The result is a **series** of **increasingly noisy samples**.

![[22_05_ForwardDiffusion.png]]

Notice that we used "latent variable" to describe each $\mathbf{x}_{t}$; this is because we are reffering to a **hidden, continuous feature space** (do not confuse this with the concept of autoencoders seen in > [[2.0 - CONVOLUTIONAL NEURAL NETWORKS#AUTO ENCODER]]).

>[!note] NOTE
>The value of $\beta_{t}$ is a **hyperparameter**.
>Furthermore, this can either be a **fixed value** $\forall t$, or it can **vary based on a schedule** over the $T$ timesteps; this schedule can be *linear*, *quadratic*, *cosine* etc. 
>The original **authors** utilised a **linear schedule** increasing from $\beta_{1}=10^{-4}$ to $\beta_{T}=0.02$.

From the definition above, it may look like we need to **compute every intermediate step** to reach the result at noise level $T$.
In reality, we can **efficiently compute** the result of this diffusion process, without having to go though each intermediate calculation.
To start, recall that we can **sample values** from a normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$ as follows:
$$
	x = \mu + \sigma \epsilon 
$$
With $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$.
Thus, for $t=1,\dots,T$ we have:
$$
	\mathbf{x}_{t} = \sqrt{ 1-\beta_{t} }\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \sqrt{ \beta_{t} }\epsilon_{t-1}
$$
We now define:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\alpha_{t} = 1 - \beta_{t} & & \bar{\alpha}_{t} = \prod_{s=0}^t \alpha_{s}
	\end{align*}
$$
So we can rewrite:
$$
	\mathbf{x}_{t} = \sqrt{ \alpha_{t} }\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \sqrt{ 1-\alpha_{t} }\epsilon_{t-1}
$$
By substituting the expression for $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ we obtain:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbf{x}_{t} &= \sqrt{ \alpha_{t} } (\sqrt{ \alpha_{t-1} }x_{t-2} + \sqrt{ 1-\alpha_{t} }\epsilon_{t-2}) + \sqrt{ 1-\alpha_{t} }\epsilon_{t-1} \\ \\
		&= \sqrt{ \alpha_{t}\alpha_{t-1} }x_{t-2} + \underbrace{ \sqrt{ \alpha_{t}(1-\alpha_{t-1}) }\epsilon_{t-2} }_{ X } + \underbrace{ \sqrt{ 1-\alpha_{t} }\epsilon_{t-1} }_{ Y }
	\end{align*}
$$
We notice that:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		X &\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\alpha_{t}(1-\alpha_{t-1})I) \\ \\
		Y &\sim \mathcal{N}(0,(1-\alpha_{t})I)
	\end{align*}
$$
We thus have:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		Z &= X+Y \sim \mathcal{N}(0,[\alpha_{t}(1-\alpha_{t}) + (1-\alpha_{t})]I) \\ \\
		&= \mathcal{N}(0, 1-\alpha_{t}\alpha_{t-1})
	\end{align*}
$$
So it follows:
$$
	Z = 0 + \sqrt{ 1-\alpha_{t}\alpha_{t-1} }\epsilon_{t-2}
$$
Finally, we obtain:
$$
	\mathbf{x}_{t} = \sqrt{ \alpha_{t}\alpha_{t-1} }x_{t-2} + \sqrt{ 1-\alpha_{t}\alpha_{t-1} }\epsilon_{t-2}
$$
>[!idea] OBSERVATION
>We can **keep repeating this process** to reach a **closed form** expression for $\mathbf{x}_{t}$.

This closed form expression is:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbf{x}_{t} &= \sqrt{ \alpha_{t}\alpha_{t-1}\dots\alpha_{1} }x_{0} + \sqrt{ 1-\alpha_{t}\alpha_{t-1}\dots\alpha_{1} }\epsilon \\ \\
		&= \sqrt{ \bar{\alpha}_{t} }\mathbf{x}_{0} + \sqrt{ 1-\bar{\alpha}_{t} }\epsilon
	\end{align*}
$$
>[!note] NOTE
>What we showed above is known as **reparametrisation trick**, and it allowed us to obtain a closed form expression which we can use to **directly compute any** $\mathbf{x}_{t}$ without having to go through the entire process.
## REVERSE DIFFUSION
The **forward step** let us go from the original image $\mathbf{x}_{0}$ **up to a complete noise image**.
What we really **want to know**, though, is the **reverse process**; that is, how we can go **from noise to an image**.

This process can be described in a **similar way**, but in the **opposite direction**:
$$
	p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}) = p(\mathbf{x}_{T}) \prod_{t=1}^T p(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
$$
Where $p(\mathbf{x}_{T})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{T};0,I)$; that is, the process should ideally **start** from an **isotropic Gaussian noise image** (meaning the noise follows the same distribution along *all possible directions* starting from the origin).

Notice that reversing the forward process requires finding the **"opposite" conditional distribution** $p(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t})$.

>[!idea] MOTIVATION
>This would let us **gradually remove the noise** until we end up with an **image** from the **real data distribution** $\mathbf{x}_{0}$.

The problem, of course, is that we do **not know this distribution**: thus, we try to **estimate it** using a **neural network**.

![[22_06_ReverseDiffusion.png]]

For this estimation, we replace the original unknown distribution $p(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t})$ with a **parametric** one, as follows:
$$
	p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
$$
Where $\theta$ denotes the **network's parameters**.

Because we are dealing with **Gaussian distributions**, $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t})$ can be fully **described** by a **mean** and a **variance**, and so we can parameterise it as:
$$
	p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t), \Sigma_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t) ) \text{ , } t=1,\dots,T-1
$$
With $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{T})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{T};0,I)$.

>[!note] ASSUMPTION
>We can make an **assumption** for our model (and **make it simpler**) and decide that the **variance is not a parameter** the network needs to learn (this same assumption was made in the original paper, but note that later works decided that the model should also learn the variance).
>As we will see below, this **greatly simplifies** the **derivation** of the **loss function**.

We thus set $\Sigma_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t):=\beta_{t}I$ for some $\beta_{t}$ that **doesn't depend** on $\theta$.
## LOSS FUNCTION
So far we have discussed **how** the **diffusion** processes **work**, but currently we **do not** even have a **precise idea** of **_what_ our network** should be **predicting**.

>[!idea] FIRST INTUITION
>We said in the introduction that we want to **train the network** to **reverse the diffusion process**: this leads us to assume that the network's **output** might be the **noise** that we need to **remove from the input** to obtain a **real image**.

It turns out that this is indeed true, but let us give a rigorous mathematical argument as to why.

First of all, assume that the **distribution of images generated** by our network after the full **reverse process** is given by $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})$.

Instinctively, we might think of taking the **expected log likelihood** over $\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim q(\mathbf{x})$ of this distribution and **maximising it**; that is, since $\mathbf{x}_{0}$ is a **valid image**, as it is sampled from the target distribution, we want our **network's probability** of obtaining it to be **high**.
Because we are in a deep learning context, we prefer **minimising the negative** log likelihood, but there is a catch.
The **problem** with this approach is that, to estimate the distribution of $\mathbf{x}_{0}$ in the reverse process, we need to **keep track of all the T steps before**.

Instead, we can find **another function** that we **_can_ optimise** to act as an **upper bound** for it.
Thus, we may consider the following inequality:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		-\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0}) \leq& -\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0}) + D_{\text{KL}} ( q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{0}) || p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{0}) ) \\ \\
		&= -\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0}) + \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{1:T}\sim q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{0})} \left[  \log \frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{0})}  \right]
	\end{align*}
$$
Notice that we added the **KL Divergence** (recall that we have discussed entropy and KL divergence in > [[1.0 - NEURAL NETWORKS INTRODUCTION#CROSS-ENTROPY LOSS]]) between the **forward process** and the **reverse process** to the right-hand side, so that it is always greater than or equal to our log likelihood.
Indeed, **this** function **_can_ be optimised**.

Notice that choosing to **minimise this other function** has an interesting implication:

>[!idea] FURTHER INSIGHT
>By **minimising** the **KL divergence** between the **two processes**, we are effectively **forcing the network** to **learn a trajectory** that **matches** the **forward process** as closely as possible, thus **accurately reversing it**.

This intuition may be better understood by looking at the image below:

![[22_07_MinimiseDKL.png]]

Now let us show why this addition is also *mathematically* convenient.
First, we apply Bayes' rule to the numerator of the log's argument:
$$
	p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{0}) = \frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T}) p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})} = \frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})} = \frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})}
$$
Substituting this back into the logarithm gives us:
$$
	\log \left( \frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{0})} \right) = \log \left( \frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{0})}{ \frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})} } \right) 
	= \log \left( \frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})} \right) + \log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
$$
Finally, substituting this into the original inequality and taking the expectation over $\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim q(\mathbf{x})$ we obtain:
$$
	E_{q}[-\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})] \leq E_{q}\left[  \log \frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}  \right]
$$
>[!note] NOTE
>We use $E_{q}$ to denote the expectation over $\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim q(\mathbf{x})$ and $\mathbf{x}_{1:T}\sim q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{0})$ for convenience, to avoid cluttering equations with symbols.

Notice that the distributions inside the logarithm are the **forward process** and **reverse process** we defined in the paragraphs above. We thus have:
$$
	\log \frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})} = \log \frac{\prod_{t=1}^T q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})}{ p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{T}) \prod_{t=1}^T p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t})} = -\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{T}) + \sum_{t=1}^T \log \frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t})}
$$
Where we simply used properties of logarithms to obtain the last result.
Now we rewrite the above expression as:
$$
	-\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{T}) + \sum_{t=1}^T \log \frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t})} = -\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{T}) + \sum_{t=2}^T \log \frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t})} + \log \frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{x}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{1})}
$$
With some (tedious) manipulations involving Bayes' rule, we can further manipulate the above result to write it in a convenient form.

>[!note] NOTE: SKIPPED CALCULATIONS
>In short, we apply Bayes' rule to the *forward process* terms and *condition* each step on the *input image* $\mathbf{x}_{0}$. Then, by using properties of logarithms we see that some *convenient cancellations are possible*. 
>Finally, with some more algebraic manipulation, we can *rewrite* the terms in such a way that makes *KL divergence appear*.

$$
	\log \frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{0})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})} = \underbrace{ D_{\text{KL}}(q(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{0}) || p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{T}) ) }_{ L_{T} } + \left( \sum_{t=2}^T \underbrace{ D_{\text{KL}} ( q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0}) || p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t}) ) }_{ L_{t} } \right) \underbrace{ - \log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{1}) }_{ L_{0} }
$$
Finally, this is the **function** that we **_can_ optimise**.
Remember that this represents the **loss** for a **single training example**; the **true training loss** is computed by taking the **expectation** of the above expression over $\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim q(\mathbf{x})$. 
Now, we will look at each term individually and omit the expectation for convenience.

First of all, let us look at $L_{T}$.

>[!idea] OBSERVATION: DROPPING THE FIRST TERM
>We conveniently notice that $q(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{0})$ **does not depend** on the **model's parameters** $\theta$ (the forward process is *not performed by the network*), and nor does $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{T})$ (recall that it was defined as $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{T};0,I)$).
>As a result, we **can drop it entirely** as it **doesn't affect the gradients** during backpropagation.

We can thus move on to the $L_{t}$ terms (for $t=2,\dots,T$). Recall that we have:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t}) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}; \mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t), \beta_{t}I) \\ \\
		q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t}, \mathbf{x}_{0}) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}; \tilde{\mu}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0}), \tilde{\beta}_{t}I)
	\end{align*}
$$
Because we don't know how to write $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t}, \mathbf{x}_{0})$ explicitly, we use Bayes' rule to obtain:
$$
	q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t}, \mathbf{x}_{0}) = \frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_{0}) q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{0})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{0})}
$$
Now, we **know** how to express **all three distributions** on the **right hand side** of the equation thanks to the **reparametrisation trick** we presented earlier.
By expressing these in exponential form, and plugging them back into the equation for $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t}, \mathbf{x}_{0})$, we obtain:
$$
	q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t}, \mathbf{x}_{0}) \propto \exp\left(  -\frac{1}{2}\left[  \frac{(\mathbf{x}_{t} - \sqrt{ \alpha_{t} }\mathbf{x}_{t-1})^2}{\beta_{t}} + \frac{(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} - \sqrt{ \bar{\alpha}_{t-1} }\mathbf{x}_{0})^2}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} - \frac{(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\sqrt{ \bar{\alpha}_{t} }\mathbf{x}_{0})^2}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}} \right] \right)
$$
Now, we want to rewrite this expression in the following form:
$$
	\mathcal{N}(x;\mu,\sigma^2) \propto \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \frac{(x-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2} \right) = \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2}\left[  \frac{1}{\sigma^2}x^2 - \frac{2\mu}{\sigma^2}x + \frac{\mu^2}{\sigma^2} \right] \right)
$$
With some algebra, we obtain:
$$
	q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t}, \mathbf{x}_{0}) \propto \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2}\left[  \underbrace{ \left( \frac{\alpha_{t}}{\beta_{t}} + \frac{1}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \right) }_{ 1/\sigma^2 } \mathbf{x}_{t-1}^2 - \underbrace{ \left( \frac{2\sqrt{ \alpha_{t} }}{\beta_{t}}\mathbf{x}_{t} + \frac{2\sqrt{ \bar{\alpha}_{t-1} }}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\mathbf{x}_{0} \right) }_{ 2\mu/\sigma^2 }\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \underbrace{ f(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0}) }_{ \mu^2/\sigma^2 } \right] \right)
$$
This **allows** us to **find** the **mean and variance** of this distribution (notice that the last term has been written as a generic function $f$, as we don't need it for this calculation) by comparing each underbraced term with the corresponding coefficient.
We thus find:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\sigma^2 &= \tilde{\beta}_{t} = 1-\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\beta_{t} \\ \\
		\tilde{\mu}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0}) &= \frac{\sqrt{ \alpha_{t} }(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\mathbf{x}_{t} + \frac{\sqrt{ \bar{\alpha}_{t-1} }\beta_{t}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\mathbf{x}_{0}
	\end{align*}
$$
From the expression for $\mathbf{x}_{t}$ we can isolate $\mathbf{x}_{0}$:
$$
	\mathbf{x}_{t} = \sqrt{ \bar{\alpha}_{t} }\mathbf{x}_{0} + \sqrt{ 1-\bar{\alpha}_{t} }\epsilon \implies \mathbf{x}_{0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{ \bar{\alpha}_{t}}}(\mathbf{x}_{t} - \sqrt{ 1-\bar{\alpha}_{t} }\epsilon)
$$
By substituting this into the previous equation (and with some algebra) we obtain the following expression for $\tilde{\mu}_{t}$:
$$
	\tilde{\mu}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{ \alpha_{t} }}\left( \mathbf{x}_{t} - \frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\sqrt{ 1-\bar{\alpha}_{t} }} \epsilon \right)
$$
Ideally, we want our network to **learn** its $\mu_{\theta}$ to **predict the target** $\tilde{\mu}_{t}$.
We may thus set:
$$
	\mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t) := \frac{1}{\sqrt{ \alpha_{t} }}\left( \mathbf{x}_{t} - \frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\sqrt{ 1-\bar{\alpha}_{t} }} \epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t) \right)
$$
>[!idea] NETWORK'S PREDICTION
>Notice that $\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)$ is the **only prediction** that our **network can make**, as *every other parameter is fixed*.
>
>Indeed, this **confirms our initial assumption** that the network's **output** is the **noise** that **was added**: that is, if we take the noisy sample and **subtract the network's prediction** from it, we should get the **original image back**. 

Now, going back to the loss function derivation, we have **everything necessary** to write the **first piece of the loss function**.

>[!note] LOSS FUNCTION
>Because the **only parameter** that depends on $\theta$ (thanks to the assumption in the original paper that the model won't need to learn the variance) is the **mean**, it can be shown that the **KL divergence** can be **simplified** into **MSE**.

We thus have:
$$
	L_{t}  = \frac{1}{2\sigma_{t}^2} || \tilde{\mu}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}) - \mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t) ||^2
$$
Plugging the expressions found earlier we obtain:
$$
	L_{t} = \frac{(1-\alpha_{t})^2}{2\alpha_{t}(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t})\sigma_{t}^2} || \epsilon - \epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t) || ^2
$$
>[!note] NOTE: FURTHER SIMPLIFICATION
>In the **original paper**, authors found empirically that the training works better if the **scaling factor is omitted**.
>Thus, we can simplify this loss to:
>$$
>	L_{t} = || \epsilon - \epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t) ||^2
>$$

To conclude the derivation, we need to **handle the last term** of our loss: $L_{0}=- \log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{1})$.
This is the **reconstruction term** and it measures how well the model can **recover the original image** from the **first noisy step** $\mathbf{x}_{1}$.

One question that could arise when looking at $L_{0}$ is the following:

> *Why should we treat it separately? What is so special about $L_0$ compared to the other $L_t$ terms?*

>[!note] $L_{0}$ MUST BE HANDLED DIFFERENTLY
>The answer lies in the fact that, while our **diffusion** process **treats images as continuous vectors** in $\mathbb{R}^D$ (where $D$ is the number of pixels), **real images are discrete**; that is, each pixel takes an *integer value* in $[0,255]$. 

Thus, when we compute $- \log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{1})$, we need the **probability** that the **model assigns** to the **exact discrete pixel values** of the **original image** $\mathbf{x}_{0}$; recall that this probability was defined as:
$$
	p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x_{0}}|\mathbf{x_{1}}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x_{0}}; \mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1},1), \beta_{1}I)
$$
The problem is we **cannot** just **evaluate a continuous Gaussian** PDF at a single point, because its **probability would be zero**; we thus need a way to **discretise it**.

The original paper assumes that the **pixel values** are **scaled** to the **range** $[-1,1]$, and thus approximates $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{1})$ as follows:
$$
	p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{1}) = \prod_{i=1}^D \int_{\delta_{-}(x_{0}^i)}^{\delta_{+}(x_{0}^i)} \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_{\theta}^i(\mathbf{x}_{1},1), \beta_{1}I)dx
$$
Where:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\delta_{+}(x) = \begin{cases}
		\infty & x = 1 \\ \\
		x + \frac{1}{255} & x < 1
		\end{cases} & & \delta_{-}(x) = \begin{cases}
		-\infty & x = -1 \\ \\
		x - \frac{1}{255} & x > -1
		\end{cases}
	\end{align*}
$$

>[!note] INTERPRETATION
>We've seen that our network **predicts** the **mean of a pixel** (here, $\mu_{\theta}^i$).
>We thus use this prediction to **draw a Gaussian** distribution and **integrate over it** in a **tiny range** centred **around the real pixel value** $x_{0}^i$ in the original image $\mathbf{x}_{0}$.
>
>Notice that the **value** of this **integral** is **larger** if the **predicted mean** $\mu_{\theta}^i$ is **close** to the **true pixel value** $x_{0}^i$.

By **repeating** this for **every pixel** $x_{0}^i$ $i=1,\dots, D$ in the image and **multiplying the results**, we obtain the probability over the **entire image**.

However, the original paper *further simplified* this loss: instead of calculating the integral, it is **approximated by the Gaussian** probability density function times the **bin width**:
$$
	p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{1}) \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{ 2\pi\beta_{1} }} \exp \left(  -\frac{1}{2} \frac{||\mathbf{x}_{0} - \mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1},1)||^2}{\beta_{1}} \right) \cdot \frac{2}{255}
$$
Which leads to the following **loss term**:
$$
	L_{0}=- \log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{1}) \approx \frac{1}{2\beta_{1}} ||\mathbf{x}_{0} - \mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1},1)||^2 + C
$$
Both the **constant** and the **scaling factor** can be **ignored**.
Furthermore, we recall that:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbf{x}_{0} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{ \bar{\alpha}_{1} }}(\mathbf{x}_{1} - \sqrt{ 1-\bar{\alpha}_{1}\epsilon }) \\ \\
		\mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1},1) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{ \bar{\alpha}_{1} }} \left( \mathbf{x}_{1} - \frac{1-\alpha_{1}}{\sqrt{ 1-\bar{\alpha}_{1} }}\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1},1) \right)
	\end{align*}
$$
Substituting these into $L_{0}$ yields:
$$
	L_{0} \approx || \epsilon - \epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1},1) ||^2
$$
Now that we have expressions for $L_{t}$ *and* $L_{0}$, we can finally write the **loss function** for training.
$$
	L = || \epsilon - \epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1},1) ||^2 + \sum_{t=2}^T || \epsilon_{t} - \epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t) ||^2
$$
>[!idea] OBSERVATION
>Because we **omitted the scaling factors** in $L_{t}$ and $L_{0}$,  $L$ turns out to be **particularly simple**.

In fact, since both $L_{0}$ and the $L_{t}$ terms have the **same form**, we can write:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		L &= \sum_{t=1}^T || \epsilon - \epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t) ||^2 \\ \\
		&= \sum_{t=1}^T || \epsilon - \epsilon_{\theta} \left( \sqrt{ \bar{\alpha}_{t} }\mathbf{x}_{0} + \sqrt{ 1-\bar{\alpha}_{t} }\epsilon ,t \right) ||^2
	\end{align*}
$$
>[!note] NOTE
>Notice that the **loss function** presented above is the loss for a **single training example** $\mathbf{x}_{0}$ that has gone through **forward diffusion processes** of $1,2,\dots,T$ steps.
>
>Ideally, we want to use **different examples** at **different noise levels**.

Thus, our loss function should be a slightly modified version of the above expression, that takes **expectation** into consideration:
$$
	L = \mathbb{E}_{t\sim U[1,T] \text{ , }\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim q(\mathbf{x}) \text{ , } \epsilon\sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)} \left[ || \epsilon - \epsilon_{\theta} \left( \sqrt{ \bar{\alpha}_{t} }\mathbf{x}_{0} + \sqrt{ 1-\bar{\alpha}_{t} }\epsilon ,t \right) ||^2 \right]
$$

>[!idea] KEY OBSERVATION
>Notice that we **began the derivation** of the loss function by introducing the **KL divergence** between the forward and reverse **processes**, but we arrived at a result that **only depends on the _entire noise_ applied** to the sample.
>
>This may seem confusing, but it is *precisely what we wanted*, because it lets us **efficiently compute the loss without** having to **account** for **each step**.
## TRAINING
Now that we have **derived a loss function** for our **diffusion model**, we can finally discuss how to **train the network**.

The **training algorithm** that was proposed in the original paper is the following:

![[22_08_TrainingAlgorithm.png]]

>[!tldr] TRAINING DIFFUSION MODELS
>1. We **sample an image** $\mathbf{x}_{0}$ from the data distribution $q(\mathbf{x})$.
>2. We **sample** a **target time-step** $t$ from a **uniform distribution**; this way, we can train the network *evenly* on **different noise levels**.
>3. We **sample a Gaussian distribution** to **introduce the noise** into the image.
>4. We **feed** the **noisy image** and the **timestep** to the network. Our model will then make a **prediction** about the **noise that was added**.
>5. We **optimise** the **model weights** by performing **gradient descent** with the **loss function** derived above.
>6. **Repeat** 1-5 until convergence is reached.

It is important to notice that the original diffusion model **did not feed the timestep directly** into the network; instead, **sinusoidal positional embedding** was used to produce an **encoding for the time-steps**.

These encodings are **fixed** (there are **no learnable parameters**) and they are computed as follows:

>[!tldr] TIMESTEP EMBEDDING
>1. **Choose** the **embedding dimension** $d$ (e.g. 128 in the original paper).
>2. For **each even index** $i$ in $[0,d)$, compute:
>$$
>	\begin{align*}
>		\text{emb}[i] &= \sin\left( \frac{t}{10000^{i/d}} \right) \\ \\
>		\text{emb}[i+1] &= \cos\left( \frac{t}{10000^{i/d}} \right)
>	\end{align*}
>$$

Positional embedding is also used in **transformer** models (see > [[3.1 - TRANSFORMERS]]) and will be discussed in more detail in the dedicated chapter.
For now, it may just be useful to consider the following intuition:

>[!idea] INTUITION
>By using a **multi-dimensional representation** of the time-steps we are allowing the network to **more easily capture complex relationships** between **similar time-steps**, which wouldn't be possible if we directly fed the single number to the model.

## SAMPLING AND FINAL DETAILS
It is now worth discussing **how the model operates** once **training is over**.

Remember that in the introduction we said that the core idea behind diffusion models is that they can **take small steps** to **gradually produce a good sample**. 
But wait:

> *We've seen that the network is trained to predict the **entire noise** that was added to the original image, **not** to go **through each single diffusion step**; so what **role** does the **underlying process** play?*

The **diffusion process** is **indeed important**, particularly during **sampling**; that is, when we use the model to actually **generate images**.
The sampling process is performed as follows:

![[22_10_Sampling.png]]

>[!tldr] SAMPLING
>1. We **sample random Gaussian noise** from an **isotropic distribution** (recall that this assumption was made when we presented the *reverse diffusion process*).
>2. We perform a **reverse diffusion** process of $T$ steps as follows:
> 	  1. Compute the **network's output** for the **previous noise level** ($\mathbf{x}_{t}$).
> 	  2. **Subtract** a **small fraction** of the prediction from $\mathbf{x}_{t}$ to **produce** a **slightly less noisy image** $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$.
> 	  3. **Repeat** until $t=1$.
>3. At last, a **real image** is **obtained**.

This process is shown in the figure below:

![[22_11_SamplingScheme.png]]

Immediately **subtracting the entire predicted noise** would produce highly **imperfect results**.
Instead, we **only subtract a tiny fraction** of noise from the starting image, then we **feed** the **result back into the network** and we **keep repeating** the process.

>[!idea] INTUITION
>As introduced at the *beginning of the paragraph* and also at > [[#REVERSE DIFFUSION]], the idea is that **taking one small step at a time** allows the model to **correct itself**, follow a **more stable trajectory** and **produce better results**.

Because we have gone over *many concepts*, it may be useful to summarise them in simple terms:

>[!tldr] DIFFUSION MODELS - SUMMARY
>1. **Forward diffusion** is used to **produce training examples** for the model.
>2. **Training** is done by **comparing** the **predicted noise** to the noise that was **actually applied**.
>3. **Inference** (*sampling*) consists in **mimicking** a **reverse diffusion process** that **gradually reduces noise** to **produce a real image**.
