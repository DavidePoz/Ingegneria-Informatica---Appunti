# LANGUAGE MODELS
Before we begin to discuss **transformer models**, it is worth taking a brief detour and look at **language models**.

>[!def] LANGUAGE MODEL
>A **language model** models the **probability distribution** over a **sequence** of **discrete tokens**:
>$$ \mathbf{x} = (x_{1},\dots,x_{T}) $$
>Where each **token** $x_{t}$ takes a **value** from a **vocabulary** $\mathcal{V}$.

These **tokens** can be **words**, single **characters** or **raw bytes** depending on the model.
Furthermore, the **last token** in a sequence needs to **signals its end**: we thus denote it with the `<EOS>` symbol.

Now, one may wonder what *"modelling the probability distribution of a sequence"* means.
Formally, we define this probability as follows:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		p(\mathbf{x}) = p(x_{1},\dots,x_{T}) &= \prod_{t=1}^T p(x_{t}|x_{1},\dots,x_{t-1}) \\ \\
		&= p(x_{1})\cdot p(x_{2}|x_{1})\cdot p(x_{3}|x_{1},x_{2})\dots
	\end{align*}
$$
Notice how we are computing the **probability** that the **first token** is $x_{1}$, then the probability that the **second token** is $x_{2}$ given that the **first was** $x_{1}$, and so on.
To understand this better, consider the following example:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		p(\text{The dog ran away <EOS>}) = &p(\text{The})p(\text{Dog}|\text{The})p(\text{ran}|\text{The dog}) \\ \\
		&p(\text{away}|\text{The dog ran})p(\text{<EOS>}|\text{The dog ran away})
	\end{align*}
$$

>[!idea] OBSERVATION
>Intuitively, we are **predicting the next token** in a sequence, **given** the fact that we already have the **previous tokens**
## TRANSFORMERS AND LLMs
The nature of the problem seems to suggest that we should use some **sequence-based model architecture**, such as **LSTM** (see > [[3.0 - SEQUENCE AND GRAPH-BASED NNs#LONG SHORT-TERM MEMORY]]); but we should also notice that **language modelling** comes with another **important detail**: a word's **meaning** may **change depending** on **other words in the sentence**.
Consider the following two examples:

>[!example] EXAMPLES
>1. She poured water from the pitcher to the cup until **it** was **_full_**.
>2. She poured water from the pitcher to the cup until **it** was **_empty_**.

Notice that in the first sentence, "*it*" refers to the *cup*; but in the second sentence *it* refers to the *pitcher*: this change simply depends on the fact that *full* was substituted by *empty*.

>[!idea] OBSERVATION
>**Meaning** is a **result** of **relationship between words**.

This problem can be tackled with what is referred to as the **"attention mechanism"** (we will discuss exactly how it works in the paragraphs below. See > [[#ATTENTION]]), which was indeed applied to LSTM and other RNN architectures to **improve the quality** of their **predictions**.

>[!idea] MOVING FORWARD
>The **attention mechanism** improves the quality of a language model, but **LSTMs** are inherently **slow** due to the fact that training requires processing **one token at a time**.
>
>*Could we design a neural network architecture that makes **use** of this **attention mechanism**, but that also allows for **better parallelisation**?*

Indeed we can, and such architecture is exactly the **transformer**, which **enabled** the development of **modern LLMs** (*large language models*).

The main idea behind these models was to **process the entire sequence at once** (hence allowing parallelisation that wasn't possible with LSTMs) and, for **each token** in the sequence, **compute its "attention"** with respect to **all the other tokens**.

To understand just how **important** the attention mechanism is, consider that **Google** researchers almost used the term as the **name for their 2017 model**.
Even though they opted for a different name (*transformer*), the **original paper** **_Attention is all you need_** (probably one of the most well-known papers in the field) effectively reflects this importance.

>[!note] CURIOSITY: ORIGIN OF THE NAME "TRANSFORMER"
>Vaswani, one of the **researchers**, has said:
>> *“I argued we were transforming representations, but that was just playing semantics.”*
# THE TRANSFORMER MODEL
Now that we've given some information about the historic background behind **transformers** and their importance for LLMs, we can finally start diving into **how they work** in more detail.

Particularly, let us begin by taking a look at the **transformer architecture** (shown in the figure below) to build a *general understanding* of the **flow of information through the network**.

![[31_01_TransformerArchitecture.png|550]]

For a start, we immediately notice that the *core* of these networks comprises **two main blocks**: an **encoder** (left) and a **decoder** (right).

To understand why we need both, imagine we're dealing with a **sequence to sequence problem**, like **translation**.
Particularly, suppose our **input sequence** is `[How, are, you?, <EOS>]` and we want to **output** the **italian translation** `[Come, stai?, <EOS>]`.

Ideally, we want to train an **encoder** to produce an **information-rich latent representation** of the **input**, so that the **decoder** can **leverage it** to produce a **relevant/accurate prediction**.
This process may seem familiar, and indeed the idea behind the **transformer encoder** shares some **similarities** with **autoencoders** (which we discussed in > [[2.0 - CONVOLUTIONAL NEURAL NETWORKS#AUTO ENCODER]]). 

To better understand the **role** of the **encoder**, let us go back to the **language translation** example proposed above, and let us consider the following observation: **input** and **output** are **naturally represented** in **different languages**.
At this point, the **role** of the **encoder-decoder architecture** should become clearer:

>[!idea] ENCODER - DECODER: INTUITION
>The **encoder** produces a **latent space representation** that **does not depend** on the **particular languages** of the input and the output.
>This allows the **network** to **learn concepts** in a **space** that **encapsulates their inherent semantics**, without being constrained by one language in particular.
>Then, clearly, we also need a **decoder** to **_"convert"_** the outputs back to **natural language**.

As for our comparison with autoencoders seen in the CNN chapter, let us clarify the following detail:

>[!note] NOTE: AUTOENCODERS AND TRANSFORMER ENCODERS
>While **autoencoders** typically **compress inputs** to **reduce their dimensionality**, transformer **encoders** learn a **new representation** for the inputs that **retains positional and contextual information**.
>
>Still, in **both cases**, the **new representation** serves to allow the **network** to **learn complex features more easily**.
## ENCODER
Now that we have a **general picture** of the transformer architecture, we can discuss in more detail how the **encoder** and **decoder** blocks actually work.
Let us start with the **encoder**.

![[31_02_EncoderDecoder.png|350]]

The **encoder** takes the **embedded input sequence** (that is, each **token** is **embedded in a vector**. Furthermore, the sequence is also injected with **positional information** thanks to **positional encoding**) and **processes** it through a **stack of layers** (notice the $N\times$: we can stack as many blocks as needed).

>[!note] ENCODER BLOCKS
>Each block consists of:
>1. An **attention layer**, which allows **every token** to **look at** all the **others** to build a **context-aware representation**.
>2. A **feed-forward network**, which **refines** the information-rich representation produced by the attention layer.

Notice that we're only giving a brief description of the role of the attention mechanism, which we will discuss more thoroughly in > [[#ATTENTION]].
## DECODER
Now, understanding the **decoder** is a bit more **complex**, particularly because we need to distinguish the *input-feeding process* for **inference** from that used during **training**.

It is probably easier if we go over the **training** process first; and again in the context of *language translation* that we used in the example above.
To this end, we should start by addressing what we mean with the **"shifted right"** note on the **decoder input**.

The output that is *"shifted right"* is the **target output** that is associated with a particular input. Thus, during the **training** phase, we take the **target outputs**, **right-shift** them and then **feed them** to the network. 
In our case the target output was `[Come, stai?, <EOS>]`; shifting it to the right means **dropping the \<EOS\> token** and **adding** an additional **special token to the front**, thus obtaining `[<SOS>, Come, stai?]` (this special token is **Start Of Sentence**).
To understand the reason behind this shift, consider the two next to each other:

| POS. IN SEQ. | DECODER INPUT | DECODER TARGET |
| :----------: | :-----------: | :------------: |
|      1       |    `<SOS>`    |     `Come`     |
|      2       |    `Come`     |    `stai?`     |
|      3       |    `stai?`    |    `<EOS>`     |
>[!idea] OBSERVATION: INPUT-TARGET ALIGNEMENT
>Notice that `<SOS>` is aligned with `Come`, `Come` with `stai?`, and `stai?` with `<EOS>`.
>
>That is, **after** the **right shift**, each **input token** is correctly **aligned** to its **target prediction** in the **output** sequence.

When the input has been fed to the network, the **decoder** computes a **masked attention**, then runs the output through **another attention layer**, which also receives the **encoder output**.
Because this layer computes attention between **both** encoder and decoder representations, we also call it **_cross attention_**.
Then, the output is **refined** by a **feed-forward layer** and a **normalisation layer**.
Finally, after $N$ **decoder blocks** (these can be stacked together just like the encoder blocks), a **linear layer** equipped with the **softmax activation function** generates the **output prediction**.

Before looking at how the decoder operates during inference, let us clarify a few details:

>[!note] DECODER - MASKED ATTENTION AND CROSS ATTENTION
>- **MASKED ATTENTION**: Recall that, unlike RNNs, the **transformer training** process looks at the **whole sequence at once**. 
>  Masked attention thus serves as a way to **prevent the network** from **looking at future tokens**. That is, by **applying a mask** we can **ensure** that **attention** is computed with respect to **past tokens** only: the model must learn to predict new tokens from the previous ones; looking at future tokens would be "cheating" (see the > [[#MASKED ATTENTION]] paragraph below).
>- **CROSS ATTENTION**: Computing **attention** between the **encoder outputs** and the **decoder's intermediate representations** allows the network to **learn which parts** of the **input** sequence are **relevant** to compute the **output** (this mechanism is better explained in the > [[#QUERY - KEY - VALUE IDEA]] and > [[#SELF ATTENTION VS CROSS ATTENTION]] paragraphs below).

Furthermore, notice that **every encoder** block receives the **output of the last encoder**, as shown below:

![[31_03_MultipleEncodersDecoders.png]]

Now we can finally move on to **inference**, where the information flow is slightly different.
Particularly, once the **encoder** has produced its **latent space representation**, we need to **query it** (again, see > [[#QUERY - KEY - VALUE IDEA]] below for details) to **retrieve information** that we need to **produce the next token**.

The **inference** process thus looks as follows:

>[!tldr] DECODER - INFERENCE
>1. We **start** by **feeding \<SOS\>** to the **decoder**.
>2. The **cross-attention mechanism** retrieves **information** from the **encoder**. 
>3. This information is used to generate the **next token**, which is **appended to \<SOS\>**.
>4. We **repeat the steps**, but we **feed the whole sequence generated so far** instead of just \<SOS\>.
>5. We **keep generating** new **tokens until \<EOS\>** is outputted by the decoder.
## RECAP - TRAINING AND INFERENCE
At this point, it might be useful to recap a few **key differences** between the training and inference processes:

>[!tldr] INFERENCE VS TRAINING
>**TRAINING**
>1. **Sequence processing**: the **encoder** receives the **entire source sequence**, the **decoder** receives the **right-shifted target sequence**.
>2. **Masking**: necessary to **prevent** the model from **looking at future tokens**.
>3. **Output**: the decoder outputs a **prediction for every position simultaneously** (and thus loss is also computed across all positions at once).
>
>**INFERENCE**
>1. **Sequence processing**: the **encoder** receives the **entire source sequence**, while the **decoder** operates in a **loop** to **autoregressively generate the output**.
>2. **Masking**: not needed, because the **"future tokens" haven't been generated yet**.
>3. **Output**: the decoder outputs the **final sequence** by adding **one token at a time** (the most likely one given the sequence produced so far) **until \<EOS\>**.

Furthermore, recall that at the beginning of the chapter (see > [[#TRANSFORMERS AND LLMs]]) we presented transformers as an **alternative to LSTMs** that allows for **better parallelisation**.
Now that we know how the **encoder** and **decoder work**, we can clarify this detail:

>[!note] LSTM VS TRANSFORMER: PARALLELISATION
>**Both models** produce their **output one token at a time**, so where is the advantage of transformers?
>
>The answer is in the **training phase**: while LSTMs need to *process one token at a time*, *even during training*; **transformers** can be trained on a **whole sequence at once**.
>
>This makes Transformers **much faster to train** compared to RNNs, enabling the massive scale of modern LLMs.

Though we should also notice that this comes at a cost: **memory required** for **transformer attention** scales **quadratically with sequence length** $T$ ($O(T^2)$, see > [[#ATTENTION]] paragraph below), whereas LSTM memory scaled linearly ($O(T)$).
# ATTENTION
So far, we have gone over the general **architecture** of the **transformer model**, and we have built a basic understanding of the **attention mechanism** as a way to **capture semantic relationships inside sequences**, but without properly discussing **how**.
Because this idea is so important for a complete understanding of transformers, it is worth dedicating a few paragraphs entirely to this concept.

For a start, let's recall what we want to achieve with the attention mechanism:

>[!idea] (SELF) ATTENTION MECHANISM
>We want **each token** in the **sequence** to **_"understand"_ the relevance** of **all other tokens to itself**.
>

That is, we want each token to **look at all the others** and **decide which** ones are **relevant** and which ones **are not**.
Consider the following example:
![[31_04_AttentionExample.png]]
We can think of **color intensity** as the **strength** of the **relationship between words**.

>[!note] NOTE
>We can see that the **word _"it"_** has:
>- **strong connections** with the words **_"the"_** and **_"animal"_**: we will associate a **high attention score** to these relationships.
>- a **weak connection** with the word **_"because"_**: this will be associated with a **low attention score**.
>- **(almost) no connections** with the **other words**: we thus want to associate these with **even lower attention scores**.

The figure showed the attention that **"it" pays to other tokens**.
Clearly, the same can be done for **all other tokens**, resulting in something like:

![[31_05_AttentionExample2.png]]

>[!note] NOTE
>What we presented here is the idea behind **self-attention**.
>Transformers also implement what we call **cross-attention**: though the core idea behind it remains unchanged, there are some differences that we discuss in > [[#SELF ATTENTION VS CROSS ATTENTION]].

With this in mind, we will go over the **main idea behind attention**, move on to **how** it is **implemented** in the **transformer model**, and, finally, we will discuss some **further details** about **multi head attention** and **masked attention**.
## QUERY - KEY - VALUE IDEA
In the [[#DECODER]] paragraph we have hinted at the fact that, ideally, we want the **encoder** to produce some kind of **information-rich representation** which then gets **queried** by the **decoder** to **generate tokens**.

The **mixing between encoder and decoder representations** is the idea behind **cross-attention** (again, see > [[#SELF ATTENTION VS CROSS ATTENTION]]), but the **concept** of **"querying for information"** is the **core intuition** behind the **whole implementation of the attention mechanism** in **transformers**. 
For this reason, we begin by discussing this idea first:

>[!note] GENERAL IDEA
>We want to **submit a query** $Q$ for **each token** in the sequence, **match them against** a **set of keys** $K$, and **retrieve values** $V$ associated with **keys that align with the query**.

To formalise this, we first **associate** each **token** with **three vectors**:
1. **QUERY** $\mathbf{q}$ Represents the token's "question" about **what information** it is **looking for** from the **rest of the sequence**.
2. **KEY** $\mathbf{k}$ Acts as a **label** (descriptor) that tells the other tokens **what kind of information** is associated with this particular token.
3. **VALUE** $\mathbf{v}$ Is the **actual information** associated with the token.

![[31_06_QKVExample.png]]

We can think of this mechanism as something analogous to **information retrieval** in **search engines**. 
Indeed, the idea is very much similar, with the only difference given by the fact that we use a **more "abstract"** and **"smooth" notion** of **_retrieving_ an object**: we **retrieve information** if the **associated key** is **_similar_ to our query**.
At this point, the following intuition should come up naturally:

>[!idea] ATTENTION AS SIMILARITY
>If the **similarity between keys and queries** acts as a way to **select relevant information**, and this was exactly the idea we wanted to implement with the attention mechanism; then **attention must be a measure of this similarity**. 

With this in mind, let us take a look at how the attention mechanism works:

>[!tldr] ATTENTION MECHANISM
>1. **Compute similarity** between a given **query** $\mathbf{q}_{i}$ and **every key** $\mathbf{k}_{j}$ to produce a score of the **relevance of each token** $j$ to token $i$.
>2. **Normalise** these scores with the **softmax function** to turn them into a **probability distribution**.
>3. **Aggregate** the **values** by **weighting them** with the **normalised scores**.
>   
>At the end, the result is a **new representation** for token $i$ that **mixes information** from the **most relevant tokens** in the sequence.

Particularly, we use the **dot product** to determine the **similarity between queries and keys**.
Thus, given a **single query vector** $\mathbf{q}_{i}$, we compute its **attention** to **all other tokens in the sequence** as follows:

>[!def] ATTENTION (SINGLE QUERY)
>$$ \begin{align*}
>	\text{Attention}(\mathbf{q}_{i},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}) &= \sum_{j} \text{softmax}\left( \frac{\mathbf{q}_{i}\cdot \mathbf{k}_{j}}{\sqrt{ d_{k} }} \right)\mathbf{v}_{j} \\ \\
>	&= \text{softmax}\left( \frac{\mathbf{q_{i}}\mathbf{K}^\top}{\sqrt{ d_{k} }} \right)\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
>\end{align*}$$
>Where $\mathbf{k}_{j}$ and $\mathbf{v}_{j}$ are the **key and value** associated with the $j$-th **token**, and the **index** $j$ runs **through** the entire **sequence**.

Notice that the second equation was simply obtained by **combining** all **key** and **value vectors** in **matrices** ($\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$) to introduce a *more compact notation*; and that we *scale* by a *factor* of $\sqrt{ d_{k} }$ where $d_{k}$ is the *dimension* of the *key* and *query* vectors.

The above definition gives us the **attention** for a **single query vector** $\mathbf{q}_{i}$.
If **all queries are available**, we can **group them** in a matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ just like we did with keys and values:

>[!def] ATTENTION (ALL QUERIES)
>$$ \text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}) = \text{softmax}\left( \frac{\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top}{\sqrt{ d_{k} }} \right)\mathbf{V} $$

This way, the result of $\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$ is what we call **attention matrix**, whose entries $a_{ij}$ are the **strengths** with which **token _i_ attends to token _j_**.

![[31_07_AttentionMatrix.png]]

>[!note] NOTE: WE NEED A MASK
>Because we **do not want** a **token** $i$ to **look at future tokens** $j>i$, the training phase requires the use of a **mask** (as already briefly introduced in > [[#DECODER]]).
>
>This introduces the need for a **masked attention layer** (see > [[#MASKED ATTENTION]] paragraph below) in the **decoder blocks**.
## SELF ATTENTION VS CROSS ATTENTION
In the previous paragraph we went over the **general idea** behind the **attention mechanism** and **how it is computed**, but we **haven't specified** how **queries, keys and values** are **obtained**.

>[!note] SELF-ATTENTION AND CROSS-ATTENTION
>Recall (see figure at the start of > [[#THE TRANSFORMER MODEL]]) that there are **two kinds** of **attention layers**.
>1. **Self-attention** layers, that compute **attention** entirely within **either** the **encoder _or_** the **decoder** (hence, the *"self"* prefix).
>2. **Cross-attention** layers, that compute **attention** on **encoder outputs _and_ decoder intermediate representations** (hence, *"cross"*). 

The fundamental difference between them lies in **where Q, K, and V come from**, which also determines their **dimensions**.
To discuss this, let us define some variables first:
- $T$: **sequence length**.
- $d_{\text{model}}$: dimension of the **input embeddings** (512 in the original paper).
- $d_{k}$: dimension of the **query** and **key vectors**.
- $d_{v}$: dimension of the **value vectors**.

Let us begin with the **self-attention layers** and an **input sequence** $\mathbf{X}\in \mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{\text{model}}}$; notice that $\mathbf{X}$ can be the **encoder input**, a **target sequence** for the **decoder** during **training**, or the **sequence generated so far** by the **decoder** during **inference**.

To **compute queries, keys and values** we simply **apply weight matrices** to this sequence; we thus have:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbf{Q} &= \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{k}} \\ \\
		\mathbf{K} &= \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{k}}\\ \\
		\mathbf{V} &= \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{v}}
	\end{align*}
$$
Notice that $\mathbf{W}^Q,\mathbf{W}^K\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{k}}$, and $\mathbf{W}^V\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{v}}$.
The **weight matrices map each component of the embedding** to **vectors** that represent the **query**, **key**, and **value** associated with that **particular representation**. 
By **repeating this** for **each token**, we can **map** the **whole sequence** to the **target** $(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V})$ **space**: this means that the **network learns** the **mappings** between **embeddings** and **queries, keys and values**.

>[!idea] OBSERVATION: LEARNING AN EFFICIENT MAPPING
>Because the **values** are **retrieved** based on the **similarity between queries and keys**, the **attention** mechanism essentially **forces the network** to **learn** this mapping in such a way that **encapsulates relationships** between **concepts** (that is, **similar concepts** will be associated with **keys** in **similar directions**).

Now let us look at the **dimension** of the **attention matrix**:
$$
	A = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{k}}\cdot\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}\times T} = \mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
$$
>[!note] NOTE: SELF-ATTENTION IS A SQUARE MATRIX
>Entries $a_{ij}$ are the **attention values** of **token _i_  _to_ token _j_**.
>Equivalently, the **i-th row** is the **attention vector** associated with the **i-th token**.
>
>Note that this matrix is **not symmetric**: we may have $a_{ij}\ne a_{ji}$.

Finally, because we defined $T$ as the **length** of the **sequence** that is **fed** to the **self-attention layer**, we should clarify the following detail:

>[!note] NOTE: MATRIX SHAPE IN ENCODER AND DECODER BLOCKS
>- **ENCODER**: 
>  The matrix has **simply** shape $T\times T$, where $T$ is the **input sequence length**.
>- **DECODER**: 
>  During **training** (where the entire sequence is fed at once), the attention matrix has shape $T_{\text{target}}\times T_{\text{target}}$, where $T_{\text{target}}$ is the **target output sequence length**. 
>  During **inference** (where the sequence is **generated one token at a time**), the attention matrix **grows in size** at **each step**: $A\in \mathbb{R}^{T_{t}\times T_{t}}$ with $t=1,\dots,T_{max}$ and $T_{1}=1$.

Now, let us do the **same analysis** for the **cross-attention layers**; but let us point out the **main difference** with self-attention first:

>[!note] NOTE: MAIN DIFFERENCE
>While in **self-attention** we have **queries, keys and values** that all **come from the same source**, when computing **cross-attention**:
>- **Queries** come from the **decoder**.
>- **Keys** and **values** come from the **encoder**.

Let the **encoder output** be $\mathbf{X}_{\text{enc}}\in \mathbb{R}^{T_{\text{enc}}\times d_{\text{model}}}$ and let the **decoder intermediate representation** be $\mathbf{X}_{\text{dec}}\in \mathbb{R}^{T_{dec}\times d_{\text{model}}}$.
The **projections** are now:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbf{Q} &= \mathbf{X}_{\text{dec}}\mathbf{W}^Q \in \mathbb{R}^{T_{\text{dec}}\times d_{k}} \\ \\
		\mathbf{K} &= \mathbf{X}_{\text{enc}}\mathbf{W}^K \in \mathbb{R}^{T_{\text{enc}}\times d_{k}} \\ \\
		\mathbf{V} &= \mathbf{X}_{\text{enc}}\mathbf{W}^V \in \mathbb{R}^{T_{\text{enc}}\times d_{v}}
	\end{align*}
$$
Notice that the **weights matrices** have the **same shapes** as before ($\mathbf{W}^Q,\mathbf{W}^K\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{k}}$ and $\mathbf{W}^V\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{v}}$), but **now** the **queries matrix** has a **different sequence length** than the **keys and values**.
As a result, the **cross attention matrix** has the following **dimensions**:
$$
	C = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{T_{\text{dec}}\times T_{\text{enc}}}
$$
Because $T_{\text{dec}}\ne T_{\text{enc}}$, we now have:

>[!note] NOTE: CROSS-ATTENTION IS A RECTANGULAR MATRIX
>The cross-attention matrix is **rectangular** and **row** $i$ tells us how much the $i$-th **decoder-generated token** attends to **each token** in the **encoder generated representation**.

Just like we did before, we should also clarify a detail about $T_{\text{dec}}$:

>[!note] NOTE: DECODER SEQUENCE LENGTH
>- During the **training phase** we have $T_{\text{dec}}=T_{\text{target}}$, which means we have **_target_ rows**, each with an **attention score** for **each token** in the **representation** generated by the **encoder**.
>- During **inference**, $T_{\text{dec}}=T_{t}$: it **increases** as we **generate new output tokens**. Thus, each time a **new token** is **generated** we **add one row** with the **attention scores** with respect to all the **encoder generated tokens**.

The notes about the **self-attention** and the **cross-attention** **matrices' dimensions** are **key** to understand the **difference** between the **two kinds** of **attention**:

>[!tldr] SELF-ATTENTION VS CROSS-ATTENTION
>- **SELF-ATTENTION** computes **attention scores within a single sequence**. 
>  Intuitively, this acts as a way to produce a **representation** that **emphasises** the **relationships** between **elements within the sequence**.
>- **CROSS-ATTENTION** computes **attention scores** between the **encoder output** (which, remember, is just **another representation of the input**) and the **decoder output** that is **being generated**.
>  Intuitively, this acts as a way for the **decoder** to **look back** at the **input** sequence and **retrieve** some **relevant source information** that needs to be **carried over** to the **output**, thus making it **more aligned** (*relevant*) with the **input**.
## MULTI HEAD ATTENTION
The authors of the *Attention is all you need* paper found that, **instead** of performing a **single attention function** on $d_{\text{model}}$-dimensional keys and queries, it was **beneficial** to **perform multiple attention functions in parallel**.

In particular, **query**, **key** and **value** matrices are **mapped to** different, **lower dimensional spaces** of dimensions $d_{k}'=d_{k}/h$ and $d_{v}'=d_{v}/h$, where $h$ is the **number of times** we want to **repeat the attention mechanism**.

Each **output** is called a **"head"** and is computed as follows:

>[!def] ATTENTION HEAD
>$$ \text{Head}_{i} = \text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}_{i}^Q, \mathbf{K}\mathbf{W}_{i}^K, \mathbf{V}\mathbf{W}_{i}^V) \text{ , }i=1,\dots,h$$

Notice that $\mathbf{W}_{i}^Q,\mathbf{W}_{i}^K\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{k}'}$ and $\mathbf{W}_{i}^V\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{v}'}$.

Because **only** the **input** is **different**, and the **attention function** remains the **same**, the key takeaway of an attention head is the following:

>[!tldr] ATTENTION HEAD
>An **attention head** essentially **computes attention** in a **lower dimensional space** compared to the "standard" attention.

Now, because we **stack** $h$ of these **attention heads**, the **final output** of a **multi-head attention layer** can be computed as follows:

>[!def] MULTI-HEAD ATTENTION
>$$ \text{MultiHead}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_{1},\dots,\text{head}_{h})\mathbf{W}^O $$
>Where $\mathbf{W}^O\in \mathbb{R}^{hd_{v}'\times d_{\text{model}}}=\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{model}}}$.

>[!idea] OBSERVATION: MULTI-HEAD ATTENTION RESULT
>The **attention heads** are **concatenated together** and then **mapped back** to a $d_{\text{model}}$-dimensional **space** that extrapolates the **information carried by each head**.

In their original work, the authors employed $h=8$ and $d_{k}'=d_{v}'=d_{\text{model}}/h=64$.
Based on the definition of multi-head attention, we can notice a few important details:

>[!note] NOTE: DETAILS
>1. Because **heads** are **independent of each other**, we can perform their **computation** in **parallel**.
>2. Due to the **reduced dimension of each head**, the **total computational cost** is **similar** to that of **single-head attention** with the original **full dimensionality**.

![[31_08_MultiHeadAttentionScheme.png|200]]

Finally, it is worth taking a look at the figure below to understand **why multi-head attention** may be **beneficial**:
![[31_09_MultiHeadAttentionExample.png]]

The image (which is also shown in the original paper) displays **attention values** in **two attention heads** within the **same layer** for the example sentence.
We can clearly see that the two heads have **learned different connections**.

>[!idea] INTUITION: MULTI-HEAD ATTENTION
>Having **multiple attention heads** allows the model to **look at different representation subspaces**, which may **capture different relationships** between tokens, thus **improving** both **generalisation capabilities** and **output quality**.
## MASKED ATTENTION
In the previous paragraphs we briefly introduced the **need for a mask** during the **training phase** to prevent the **decoder** from **computing attention** with respect to **"future" tokens**.

To understand **why this would be a problem** in the first place, let us consider the following example.

>[!example] TRAINING EXAMPLE
>- **TARGET SEQUENCE**: `[I, am, fine, <EOS>]`.
>- **TRAINING INPUT**: `[<SOS>, I, am, fine]` (*right-shifted target sequence*).

>[!note] THE PROBLEM
>If we were to **generate** the **sequence** during **inference**, each time we **add a new token** we would **compute its attention to past tokens only** (e.g. when we add `am`, we would compute its attention to `<SOS>` and `I`. Token `fine` hasn't been generated yet, so `am` does not attend to it).
>
>During **training**, on the other hand, we have the **whole sequence available** and there is **nothing stopping us** from computing the **attention** that `am` pays to `fine`: the network would **"cheat"** the training process by **looking at the future**.

To **solve this problem** we **add a mask matrix** (or **causal mask**) to the **attention** matrix **before running it through softmax**:
![[31_10_MaskedAttentionScheme.png|200]]

Mathematically, this mask is defined as follows:

>[!def] CAUSAL MASK
>The **causal mask** is an **upper triangular matrix** of $-\infty$ values of the form:
>$$
>	\mathbf{M}_{\text{causal}} = \begin{bmatrix}
>	0 & -\infty & -\infty & \dots \\
>	0 & 0 & -\infty & \dots \\
>	0 & 0 & 0 & \dots \\
>	\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots
>	\end{bmatrix}
>$$

>[!idea] OBSERVATION: MASK STRUCTURE
>Recall that **each row** $i$ in the **attention matrix** contains the **attention scores** with which **token** $i$ **attends** to the **others**.
>Because we want a **token** $i$ to **only attend** to **tokens** $j\leq i$, we set $m_{ij}=-\infty$ for all **positions** where $j>i$. 

To **add it** to the **computed attention** before the **softmax function**, we simply compute **masked attention** as follows:

>[!def] MASKED ATTENTION
>$$ \text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}) = \text{softmax}\left(  \frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top}{\sqrt{ d_{k} }} + \mathbf{M}_{\text{causal}} \right)\mathbf{V} $$

Notice that, after **adding the causal mask**, the **masked positions** take $-\infty$ as their value.
Thus, their **output** after `softmax` is **applied** is **zero** (because $e^{ t }\to0$ as $t\to-\infty$).

Now, one may wonder **how we handle** the **variable length** in the **training examples**.
That is, imagine that **within a batch** we have some sequences of **length 3** and some other of **length 5**. This would **require causal masks** of **different sizes**.

To overcome this issue, we add special **padding tokens** `<PAD>` to the **end** of the **shorter sequences** so that they **all have the same length**.
Then, since we **do not want** our tokens to **attend to padding tokens**, we also define a **padding mask**:

>[!def] PADDING MASK
>It is a **square matrix** of the form:
>$$
>	\mathbf{M}_{\text{pad}} = \begin{bmatrix}
>	0 & \dots & 0 & -\infty & \dots & -\infty \\
>	0 & \dots & 0 & -\infty & \dots & -\infty \\
>	\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\
>	0 & \dots & 0 & -\infty & \dots & -\infty
>	\end{bmatrix}
>$$
>That is, we have $m_{ij}=-\infty$ where $j\geq$ **original sequence length** (equivalently, $m_{ij}=-\infty$ if `S[j] == <PAD>`, where $S$ is the *training example sequence*).

In practice, we **combine** the **causal mask** and the **padding mask** into a **single mask**:
$$
	\mathbf{M}_{\text{final}} = \mathbf{M}_{\text{causal}} + \mathbf{M}_{\text{pad}}
$$
Which will look like:
$$
	\mathbf{M}_{\text{final}} = \begin{bmatrix}
	0 & -\infty & -\infty & -\infty & \dots & -\infty \\
	0 & 0 & -\infty & -\infty & \dots & -\infty \\
	0 & 0 & 0 & -\infty & \dots & -\infty  \\
	0 & 0 & 0 & -\infty & \dots & -\infty \\
	\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\
	0 & 0 & 0 & -\infty & \dots & -\infty
	\end{bmatrix}
$$
And we **compute attention** by applying the **full mask**:
$$ 
\text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}) = \text{softmax}\left(  \frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top}{\sqrt{ d_{k} }} + \mathbf{M}_{\text{final}} \right)\mathbf{V} 
$$

## TOKENISATION AND EMBEDDING
We have been **assuming** that the **input** to the model is **already a sequence of vectors**.
In reality, the raw input is **text** (a string of characters/words); but a transformer can only operate on **numbers**. 
We thus **need a way** to **convert text into a numerical representation** so that the network can process it.

Particularly, we need to do this in **two steps**:

>[!note] NOTE: TRANSFORMING TEXT INTO VECTORS
>1. **TOKENISATION** - We kept mentioning **"tokens"** as the **individual bits** that we feed to the model; these are **obtained** by **splitting the original sequence** into **smaller units** (e.g. words or characters). 
>2. **EMBEDDING** - We then need to **map each token** to a dense **vector** that **retains its semantics**.

Let us start by discussing **tokenisation**, which includes a **sequence of steps**:

>[!tldr] TOKENISATION PIPELINE
>- **NORMALISER** - Applies **Unicode normalisation** (e.g. converting curly quotes to straight quotes), **lowercasing** (optional), **strips accents** and **removes control characters** to **reduce variance in the vocabulary**.
>- **PRE-TOKENISER** - Breaks text into **candidate pieces** by **applying basic rules** (e.g. splitting at whitespaces or applying a simple regex).
>- **TOKENISATION MODEL** - **Splits** the **pre-tokenised chunks** into **subwords** based on the **learned vocabulary** (e.g. *"unhappiness"* might not be in the vocabulary, but *"un"*, *"happy"* and *"ness"* probably are).
>- **POST-PROCESSOR** - Adds **special tokens** (e.g. `<SOS>`, `<EOS>`, `<PAD>`) and **maps each token to an integer ID** from the vocabulary.

For example, the sentence "Hello, I'm a TransFormeR!" might go through the following steps:
1. `"Hello, I'm a transformer!"`
2. `[Hello],[,],[I'm][a][transformer][!]`
3. `[Hello],[,],[I]['m][a][transform][er][!]`
4. `[<SOS>][Hello],[,],[I]['m][a][transform][er][!][<EOS>]` -> `[101,7592,1010,1005,1045,1049,1037,10938,2121,999]`

At this point, the **integer IDs** produced by the post-processor are the **input** to the **embedding layer**, which simply acts as a **lookup table**.
That is, given a **token ID** `t_ID` we compute its **embedding** as:
```
emb[t_ID] = Vocabulary[t_ID, :]
```

>[!idea] OBSERVATION: VOCABULARY
>The **vocabulary** is a **matrix** $\mathbf{E}$ of shape:
>$$ \mathbf{E}\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{vocab}}\times d_{\text{emb}}} $$
>Where $d_{\text{vocab}}$ is the **number of words** in the **vocabulary**, and $d_{\text{emb}}$ is the **embedding dimension**.

Thus, **retrieving** the **embedding of a token** consists in **reading a row** from the **vocabulary matrix**.

Furthermore, it is important to point out that in **modern transformers** the **embedding matrix** is **learned during training**.
**Early NLP** models, on the other hand, employed a **static, pre-trained embedding table**. These were obtained with methods such as **Word2Vec**, by **training** the **algorithm** on a **separate dataset** (text corpus).

>[!note] STATIC VS LEARNED EMBEDDINGS
>Because **static embeddings** are **learnt by an independent algorithm**, they may **not suit** a **specific application domain very well**.
>
>Contrarily, if we **let our model learn embeddings on its own**, there is a good chance that these will **fit the task better**.
## POSITIONAL ENCODING
After the **tokenisation and embedding**, the input sequence receives a **positional encoding addition**, which consists in **adding** a **set** of **small constants** to the word embedding vectors before feeding them to the first attention layer.

>[!note] WHY POSITIONAL ENCODING
>**Embeddings alone** are **not sufficient** to **retain positional information**.
>That is, a **word's embedding** is **always the same**, **independently** of its **position in the sentence**.
>
>Thus, we would like to **change the representation** of a token so that **it changes depending** on **_where_ it appears** in the **sentence**.

The authors of the **original paper** decided to employ **sinusoidal positional encoding** to address this issue.

>[!def] SINUSOIDAL POSITIONAL ENCODING
>For **every position** $t$ in the **input sequence** (e.g. $t=0$ for the first token, $t=1$ for the second, and so on) we **add** the following **vector** to the **token embedding**:
>$$
>	\mathbf{p}_{t} = \begin{bmatrix}
>	\sin(\omega_{1}t) \\
>	\cos(\omega_{1}t) \\
>	\sin(\omega_{2}t) \\
>	\cos(\omega_{2}t) \\
>	\vdots \\
>	\sin(\omega_{d/2}t) \\
>	\cos(\omega_{d/2}t)
>	\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}
>$$
>Where $d$ is the **embedding dimension**, and the **frequencies** $\omega_{k}$ are given by:
>$$
>	\omega_{k} = \frac{1}{10000^{2k/d}} \text{ , }k=1,\dots,d/2
>$$

The actual **encoder input** is thus given by **adding** the **word embedding vector** and the **positional encoding vector** for **each token** in the **sequence**.

Now, one may wonder **why** we should **use sinusoidal functions** of this kind.
To answer this, let us consider just **one frequency pair** at **position** $t$:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\text{PE}_{(t,2i)} &= \sin(\omega_{i}t) \\ \\
		\text{PE}_{(t,2i+1)} &= \cos(\omega_{i}t)
	\end{align*}
$$
Now, let us see **what happens** when we **shift the position** $t$ by a **fixed offset** $\Delta t$:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\text{PE}_{(t+\Delta t,2i)} &= \sin(\omega_{i}(t+\Delta t)) = \sin(\omega_{i}t)\underbrace{ \cos(\omega_{i}\Delta t) }_{ u } + \cos(\omega_{i}t)\underbrace{ \sin(\omega_{i}\Delta t) }_{ v } \\ \\
		\text{PE}_{(t+\Delta t,2i+1)} &= \cos(\omega_{i}(t+\Delta t)) = \cos(\omega_{i}t) \underbrace{ \cos(\omega_{i}\Delta t) }_{ u } - \sin(\omega_{i}t) \underbrace{ \sin(\omega_{i}\Delta t) }_{ v }
	\end{align*}
$$
Notice that $u$ and $v$ **do not depend on the position** $t$, but **only** on the **fixed offset**.
We can thus rewrite the equations as follows:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\text{PE}_{(t+\Delta t,2i)} &= u\cdot\text{PE}_{(t,2i)} + v\cdot\text{PE}_{(t,2i+1)} \\ \\
		\text{PE}_{(t+\Delta t,2i+1)} &= -v\cdot\text{PE}_{(t,2i)} + u\cdot\text{PE}_{(t,2i+1)}
	\end{align*}
$$
Equivalently, we can write:
$$
	\begin{pmatrix} 
	PE_{(t + \Delta t, 2i)} \\ 
	PE_{(t + \Delta t, 2i+1)} 
	\end{pmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} u & v \\ -v & u \end{bmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} PE_{(t, 2i)} \\ PE_{(t, 2i+1)} \end{pmatrix}
$$
This implies that we can **compute** the **positional encoding** for a **shifted position** as a **rotation** of the **"original" positional encoding**.

>[!note] IDEA BEHIND SINUSOIDAL POSITIONAL ENCODING
>The original authors **hypothesised** that this would **allow** the **model** to **more easily learn** to **attend by relative position** as **opposed** to learning **separate rules** for **each absolute position**.

In fact, let us look at how the **attention score** between a **query** $i$ and a **key** $j$ is computed:
$$
	\text{score}(i,j) = \mathbf{q}_{i}\cdot \mathbf{k}_{j}
$$
Where:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbf{q}_{i} &= \mathbf{W}^Q(\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{p}_{i}) \\ \\
		\mathbf{k}_{j} &= \mathbf{W}^K(\mathbf{x}_{j}+\mathbf{p}_{j})
	\end{align*}
$$
We thus obtain:
$$
	\text{score}(i,j) = \ldots + (\mathbf{W}^Q\mathbf{p}_{i})\cdot(\mathbf{W}^K\mathbf{p}_{j})
$$
**NOTE**: We have omitted the first three terms, because we are only interested in the last one.

>[!idea] OBSERVATION: POSITION-POSITION TERM
>Part of the **attention score** between $\mathbf{q}_{i}$ and $\mathbf{k}_{i}$ **depends on their positional encodings** ($\mathbf{p}_{i}$ and $\mathbf{p}_{j}$).
>
>Thus, it is reasonable to imagine that the fact that the **components for each frequency pair** can be expressed in terms of their **relative position** will **help the model** to rely on these, **instead of absolute positions**, thus reducing the "learning effort" (because the model learns how to handle a particular offset independently of its starting position).

Finally, we conclude this paragraph by addressing one more question: **why not let the model learn the positional encodings?**
The **boring answer** is that the original authors **tried this approach too**, but did **not notice** significant **differences in performance**, and thus opted for the idea above.

A **more interesting answer** can be understood by looking at the **heatmaps** below:

![[31_16_PositionalEncodingsHeatmaps.png]]

The heatmaps show the **positional encoding values** for a **sequence of 300 tokens** (left) and a **sequence of 3000 tokens** (right) (the position within the sequence is represented on the $y$ axis, while the $x$ axis represents the index of the embedding dimension).

Notice that the **positional encoding values** for the **"first" embedding dimensions** oscillate with **high frequencies** (observe a vertical band in the left part of the heatmaps), while the **values** for the **"late" embedding dimensions** oscillate with **much lower frequencies** (observe a vertical band in the right part of the heatmaps: colours change much less rapidly).

What is **more interesting** to us, though, is the following observation:

>[!idea] SAME PATTERN FOR DIFFERENT LENGTHS
>Even though the **two sequences** have **wildly different lengths**, the **pattern** with which the **values oscillate** is the **same in both cases**.
>
>This suggests that **sinusoidal positional encoding** is **not influenced by sequence length**, which in turn means that it will be able to **handle in the same way** even **sequences of lengths** that **were not seen during training**.
## ADD-AND-NORM AND LINEAR LAYERS
**Each layer** in the network (that is, both attention layers and feed-forward layers) is **followed** by an **_add and norm_ layer**.
We can identify each layer as a pair of a **sublayer** and an **add and norm** function. Mathematically, the **output** of these **pairs** is defined as:
$$
	\text{LayerNorm}(x + \text{Sublayer}(x))
$$
Where $\text{Sublayer}(x)$ is the **function** implemented by the **attention/feed-forward layer**.

>[!note] NOTE: RESIDUAL CONNECTIONS
>Notice that **adding the input** is simply due to the fact that **residual connections are employed** (see > [[2.0 - CONVOLUTIONAL NEURAL NETWORKS#RESNET]]).
>Furthermore, to facilitate these connections, **all sublayers** (in the original implementation) produce **outputs of dimension** $d_{\text{model}}=512$. 

As for the **layer normalisation** part, the idea behind it is very much **similar** to that of **batch normalisation** (see > [[1.2 - DEEP NEURAL NETWORKS#BATCH NORMALISATION]]), with the sole following difference:

>[!note] LAYER NORM VS BATCH NORM
>- **LAYER NORM**: Mean and variance are computed across the **channels and spatial dimensions**.
>- **BATCH NORM**: Mean and variance are computed across the **spatial and batch dimensions**.

Mathematically, this means **normalising** each **token** in the sequence as follows:
$$
	\hat{x}_{nk} = \frac{x_{nk} - \mu_{n}}{\sqrt{ \sigma_{n}^2 } +\epsilon}
$$
Where:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\mu_{n} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K x_{nk} & & \sigma_{n}^2 = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K (x_{nk}-\mu_{n})^2
	\end{align*}
$$
**NOTE**: $n=1,\dots,T$ indexes the *tokens in the sequence*, $k$ indexes the *features of each token*.

Furthermore, in addition to the attention sublayers, **each encoder** and **decoder block** contains a **fully connected feed-forward** network, which is **applied to each position** in **parallel** and with **shared weights** (that is, we apply the **same transformation** to **each token** in *parallel*).

More precisely, these feed-forward networks consist of **two linear transformations** with a **ReLU activation** function between them:
$$
	\text{FFN}(x) = \text{max}(0,xW_{1}+b_{1})W_{2} + b_{2}
$$
The original implementation employed a *feed-forward network* with $d_{\text{model}}=512$ **input neurons**, $d_{ff}=2048$ **hidden neurons** and $d_{\text{model}}$ **output neurons**.

>[!idea] INTUITION: LINEAR LAYER
>By **briefly projecting** the **self-attention output** in a **higher-dimensional space**, we can ideally **capture further nuances** about the tokens, before feeding them to the next block.
## ENCODER/DECODER-ONLY ARCHITECTURES
So far we have only discussed the "complete" **encoder-decoder architecture**, but there exist transformer models that **only employ either one or the other**.
Some examples are shown below:

![[31_11_TransformerArchitectures.png]]

In the complete **encoder-decoder** architecture, the **decoder** can generate **relevant output** thanks to the **rich representation** produced by the **encoder**.

>[!idea] STRENGTH OF ENCODER-DECODER MODELS
>This **deep interconnection** between **input representation** and **generated tokens** makes this kind of transformers **particularly good** at **sequence to sequence** tasks such as **language translation**.

As we can see from the figure above, though, **modern LLMs** (e.g. ChatGPT) are **decoder-only architectures**.
This is (mainly) due to the following reasons:

>[!note] DECODER-ONLY ADVANTAGES
>1. These models **can** be **trained** on massive amounts of **_unlabeled_ text** to **simply predict** the **next token** based on the **previous ones**.
>   This **self-supervised** objective is a lot **easier to scale** compared to the **input-output datasets** required by **standard encoder-decoder** models (producing a dataset of *user query + target output* pairs would be *practically impossible*, while *simply feeding example text* is *much easier*).
>2. By using a **single stack of decoders** we can significantly **reduce memory overhead** and **computational complexity** (one reason for this advantage is that we *do not need to keep encoder outputs in memory*, because *we do not compute cross-attention*).
>3. It's been observed that at **large scales** (10B+ **parameters**) **decoder-only** models achieve **better zero-shot performance**.

All these reasons combined make **decoder-only architectures** inherently **better** at tasks such as **Q&A**, text **summarisation**, **coding** and, perhaps more importantly, **problem solving**.

>[!idea] DECODER-ONLY: PROBLEM SOLVING
>Because these models learn to **predict the next token** with training processes that involve **enormous datasets**, they are incredibly **good** at **learning patterns**, even **subtle ones** that **humans likely can't see**.
>
>This contributes to their **zero-shot capabilities**, which in turn are **fundamental for problem solving** tasks: even when the model is presented with a **problem it's never seen before**, it may **detect** some **obscure** (that is, to us humans) **pattern** that **links it to some training examples**, thus **producing** a somewhat **plausible answer**.

Furthermore, readers may remember that the first GPT (which stands for **generative pretrained transformer**) models available to the public used to produce some answers that were **_plausible_**, but **not quite correct**; while **current models** seem to produce **much better** and **more pertinent answers**.
Indeed, these **improvements** are largely due to the **advantages** presented above.

Before discussing encoder architectures, it is worth stopping for a moment to discuss **pre-training**, a concept that is **very important** for modern LLMs.

>[!note] PRE-TRAINING VS TRAINING
>The **"training" phase** on a **huge unlabeled dataset** we mentioned above is actually what is called **pre-training**.
>
>The "actual" **training phase** comes **after pre-training** and consists in **adapting the model** to a **more specific task** using a **smaller, labeled dataset** (for this reason, it is often called **fine-tuning**).
>
>Intuitively, **pre-training** aims at **creating the "foundational intelligence"** of the model, while **training** aims at **specialising the model** for **domain-specific** performance.

Finally, **encoder-only models** also exist (e.g. **BERT**, Biderectional Encoder Representations Transformer; and **ViT**, see > [[#ViT - VISION TRANSFORMER]] below).

>[!idea] ENCODER-ONLY ARCHITECTURES
>By only keeping the **encoder stack**, we may predict these models to be particularly good at **tasks** that require **rich contextual embeddings**.

Indeed, this is true: encoder-only models are especially good at tackling problems like **sentiment analysis**, **text classification**, or **named entity recognition**, where the model **must comprehend** the **full context** of a sequence.
# TRANSFORMER EXAMPLES
Finally, we conclude this chapter by presenting **two example architectures**: **GPT-OSS**, an **open weight LLM** by OpenAI; and **ViT**, an **encoder-only architecture** that **surpassed CNNs** in **vision tasks**.
## GPT-OSS
**GPT-OSS** (2025) is the **first open weight** model that OpenAI has **released since GPT-2** (2019), and it brought several key **architectural improvements** that enhanced **efficiency**, **scalability**, and overall **performance**.

>[!note] NOTE: OPEN WEIGHT $\ne$ OPEN SOURCE
>An **open weight** model usually comes with:
>1. A **weight file** (e.g. `.safetensors` or `.gguf` format) which contains the **weights' values**.
>2. A **config file** (`config.json`) which **describes** the **model architecture** (number of layers, number of attention heads, activation functions, position embedding type, hyperparamters, etc.).
>3. The **runtime code**, which **instantiaties** the **layer graph** and **loads the weights in memory**.
>   
>It **does not** come with **training code** and **training data** (which is why such models do **not** classify as **open source**).

GPT-OSS is actually a **family of two models**:
1. **GPT-OSS-20B**
2. **GPT-OSS-120B**

The first is a **20 billion parameters model** designed for **broad accessibility**: it can be ran on **consumer GPUs** with at around 16GBs of VRAM and achieves **performance comparable to GPT-3.5**.
The second is a **120 billion parameters model** which requires **significantly** more **memory** and **computational power** (ideally, it requires GPUs with 80GB+ of VRAM, such as NVIDIA H100).

One of the **key features** of these models is that they are a **Mixture of Experts** (**MoE**).
To understand what this means, let us first look at their overall architecture (here, we are showing the 20B model variant, but the 120B has a similar architecture):

![[31_12_GPT-OSS_Architecture.png]]

>[!note] MIXTURE OF EXPERTS
>An **expert** is a **feed-forward network** (here, 2 MLPs with a SwiGLU activation function) within the main model.
>During **inference**, only a **set number of experts** is **activated per token** (4 here), **chosen** by the **expert router gate**, which is **trained** to **select the best expert** (among those available) for a **particular token**.

Clearly, this implies a **huge reduction** in the **computational cost**, because **not all parameters are active** for **all tokens** (hence why the 120B model does not require six times as much memory as the 20B variant).

In any case, **MoE** is **not** the **only difference** with the standard transformer model we have discussed in the above paragraphs.
Namely, it uses **RoPE** (**Rotary Position Embedding**) instead of traditional positional encodings; **RMSNorm** instead of layer normalisation; and, lastly, a **sliding-window attention mechanism**.
Let us discuss the *last two* (see > [[#POSITIONAL ENCODING]] for RoPE).

**RMSNorm** is employed to **reduce** the **expensive mean and variance computations** to **improve training efficiency** by using the **root mean square statistic**:

>[!def] RMSNORM
>Each **token** $x_{i}$ of the normalisation layer's input is **normalised** as follows:
>$$ \bar{x}_{i} = \frac{x_{i}}{\text{RMS}(\mathbf{x})}g_{i} \text{ , }i=1,\dots,n $$
>Where $g_{i}$ is the **gain parameter** (used to re-scale the standardised inputs, it is a *set of learnable parameters*) and:
>$$ \text{RMS}(\mathbf{x}) = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_{i}^2 } $$

**Sliding-window attention** computes **attention** on a **limited window** instead of the **whole sequence** to **save computations**, as shown below:

![[31_13_SlidingWindowAttention.png]]

>[!note] NOTE: GPT-OSS ATTENTION
>GPT-OSS **alternates** between layers with **full-context attention** and layers that employ a **sliding-window attention** mechanism restricted to **128 tokens**.
## ViT - VISION TRANSFORMER
**ViT** (*Vision Transformer*) is an **encoder-only** transformer **architecture** designed for **computer vision tasks**.

The model's **performance** was shown to be *acceptable* when trained on the *ImageNet* dataset (one million images), **great** when trained on **ImageNet-21k** (14M images), and **state-of-the-art** when pre-trained on **Google's internal JFT-300M** dataset (300M images).

One may wonder why **transformers outperform CNNs** on **large datasets**, but **fall behind** when applied to **smaller datasets**.
The answer lies in the **inductive bias** (set of assumptions that are intrinsic to the model) of the two architectures:
- **CNNs** have a **strong inductive bias** because their architecture intrinsically **assumes locality, translation invariance** (see > [[2.0 - CONVOLUTIONAL NEURAL NETWORKS#CNNs AS TRANSLATION INVARIANT, CONSTRAINED MLPs]]), and **feature hierarchy**.
- **Transformers**, on the other hand, have **weak inductive bias** because they **make no assumptions about the data**; instead, they rely on the **attention** mechanism to learn all the details.

>[!note] PERFORMANCE AND INDUCTIVE BIAS
>By making **fewer assumptions** about the **data** (*lower inductive bias*), **ViT** can **better adapt itself** to the **given task**.
>
>This means that, when the **sample size** is **too small**, the model **is not able** to **learn the basic features**; it instead **overfits** due to the **large amount of parameters**.
>
>Contrarily, when the **sample size** is **massive**, the **lack of assumptions** becomes an **advantage** that lets the model **adapt more freely to the problem**, unlike CNNs that *remain constrained*.

Particularly, we can train **vision transformers** as follows:

>[!tldr] ViT TRAINING
>1. **PRE-TRAINING** - Pre-train the model on a **large dataset** (e.g. JFT 300M) to let the **model learn features on its own**.
>2. **FINE-TUNING** - Fine-tune (*train*) the model on a **specific sub-task** to let it **adapt to the specific details**.

Now, let us take a look at **ViT's architecture**. 
As already stated, it is an **encoder-only model**, and it thus **stacks encoder blocks** like the one shown below:
![[31_14_ViT_Encoder.png|200]]
The **main problem** that the authors had to face was **figuring out how to feed images to a transformer**.
The **proposed solution** was exceptionally **simple** and **clever**:

>[!tldr] FEEDING IMAGES TO A TRANSFORMER
>1. **Split** the image into **patches**.
>2. **Flatten** the patches.
>3. Produce **lower-dimensional linear embeddings** from **each flattened patch**.
>4. Add **positional embeddings**.

![[31_15_ViT_FeedingInput.png]]

Notice that:
1. Each **patch** is **accompanied** by a **number** (label) that refers to the patch **position in the original image**.
2. In addition to the input patches, the input layer features an **additional class embedding** token. This is **randomly initialised** and its value is **learned during training** (it is not fed with the input).

To understand why the **extra class embedding** is **useful**, we first need to point out that the **embedding dimension** remains **fixed throughout the whole network**, so that **short residual connections** can be employed (that is, we simply carry the output to a further layer without the need to project it to another space).
Furthermore, we notice that the **final class prediction** is **only performed** on the basis of the **first token** of the **final encoder output**, and *not on the whole output sequence*.

>[!idea] INTUITION: CLASS EMBEDDING
>The **model learns** to use the **class embedding** token (and its equivalent in the successive layers) to **store context information** about the **entire image**.
>
>At the end, we **only need** to pass this **"_context token_"** to the **MLP head** to **predict the final class**, because it will contain all the **relevant information extrapolated from the processed patches**.
