# DEEP NEURAL NETWORKS
In > [[1.0 - NEURAL NETWORKS INTRODUCTION]] we introduced neural networks, the **perceptron** and the **multi-layer perceptron**, but a lot of the modern-day **hype** regarding AI and neural networks revolves around **deep neural networks**:

>[!def] DEEP NEURAL NETWORK (DNN)
>Neural network with **at least two hidden layers**.

Thus, DNNs are, to put it in *extremely simple terms*, a **big, scaled-up version of the MLP**.
The idea behind DNNs was **partially inspired** by **human reactions to stimuli**: there are *about ten levels between the retina and actuator muscles*.
Though in practice, as time went on, the **number of layers** in DNNs **increased** way past this threshold: the most common modern models consist of **7 to ~150** layers.

Notice that **depth** is **only one of the complexity factors** of a DNN.
The **total number of neurons**, the **amount of connections and weights** also play an important role.

![[12_00_ParameterCountEvolution.png]]

>[!idea] OBSERVATIONS
>- The **greater the number of weights**, the **more complex training is**.
>- A **large number of neurons** makes **propagation** (both forward and backwards) **more computationally expensive**.

Notice that, in the examples presented in > [[1.0 - NEURAL NETWORKS INTRODUCTION]], the neurons had *all possible connections with adjacent layers*; networks of this kind are called **fully connected DNNs** (*FCDNN*).

This "full connection" is **not a requirement**: various kinds of DNNs are **not fully connected**.
An example is given by **Convolutional Neural Networks**, which are discussed in > [[2.0 - CONVOLUTIONAL NEURAL NETWORKS]].
## WHY WE NEED "DEEP" NETWORKS
We've discussed the **universal approximation theorem** in > [[1.0 - NEURAL NETWORKS INTRODUCTION#UAT AND MULTILAYER NETWORKS]], which proves that *MLPs with one hidden layer are universal approximators*.
Notice that this is an **existence theorem**, meaning it **doesn't tell** us anything about **how to actually build** (that is, how many neurons we need) these **approximators**.

This is one of the **main reasons** why, after a period of *initial excitement* for 3-layer MLPs, **attention shifted** to **deep neural networks**: once **training algorithms were developed** and **hardware became more powerful** (see > [[1.0 - NEURAL NETWORKS INTRODUCTION#BACKPROPAGATION]]), it became **much easier** to **build DNNs** than to find the right MLP model predicted by the UAT.

>[!note] CURIOUS FACT
>In 1960 the idea of the **"grandmother cell"** was proposed: it was thought to be a *neuron that represents a complex and specific concept* that *fires when this concept is encountered*.
>
>The idea was **quickly proven false** by psychologists, but an experiment found it to be **true for DL models**.
>In 2012, a research team led by Andrew Ng and Jeff Dean fed **10 million random YouTube videos** to the **Google Brain Simulator**. It was then found that the model had developed a **peculiar neuron** which fired **only for videos containing cats**.
## EXPLODING/VANISHING GRADIENT PROBLEM
One of the main challenges that needed to be tackled to make DNNs work was the **vanishing** (and also *exploding*) **gradient problem**.

Recall that the **backpropagation** algorithm relies on the following two equations:
$$ 
\begin{align*}
	\delta^{(L)} &= \nabla_{z}J \odot f'(\text{net}^{(L)}) \\ \\
	\delta^{(l)} &= \left( w^{(l)} \delta^{(l+1)} \right) \odot f'(\text{net}^{(l)})
\end{align*} 
$$
That is, at each step, we compute the **gradient with respect to one layer's weights** and then we **keep propagating it backwards for each layer** in the network.
This introduces **two possible problems**, because we may be facing **parameter updates** that are either:
- **Excessively large**, destroying our model (*exploding gradient* problem).
- **Excessively small**, making *learning impossible* as parameters barely move on each update (*vanishing gradient* problem).

Imagine we're **starting with a low value** of $\nabla_{z}J$: as a consequence, $\delta^{(L)}$ will **also be a small number** (unless $f'(\text{net}^{(L)})$ is somehow huge); but this will also make $\delta^{(L-1)}$ a small number, and so on. 
That is, when reaching the final steps we obtain numbers that result from **long streaks of multiplications of small numbers**, thus making each $\delta^{(l)}$ **progressively smaller**.
The same reasoning applies to *excessively large numbers*.

>[!idea] SOURCES OF THE PROBLEM
>The problems can be solved in two ways, each acting on *one* of the causes:
>1. Clever **parameter initialisation**: intelligently choosing the initial weights means $w^{(l)}$ can *offset problems introduced by* $f'(\text{net}^{(l)})$.
>2. Different **choice of activation function**: the sigmoid's derivative is *practically zero* at the *two ends of its domain*, thus choosing an activation function with a *better derivative profile* can reduce the risk of facing vanishing gradients.
## PARAMETER INITIALISATION
As seen above, one way of addressing (or at least **minimising**) the issues related to the calculation of the gradient for backpropagation is through a **careful initialisation** of the weights.
We know weights are **initialised at random** before training, thus the question is: **what kind of distribution should they follow?**

>[!idea] IDEA
>We can **look at the distributions of the outputs** of each layer (which will *depend on the weights*) and **impose** that **their variances remain fixed throughout the layers**.

Let's consider the **distribution** of an **output** $o_{i}$ (of one of the *hidden neurons*) during **forward-propagation** for some **fully connected layer without non-linearities**.
With $n_{\text{in}}$ inputs (neurons of the *previous layer*) $x_{j}$ and their *associated weights* $w_{ji}$ for the considered layer, an output is given by:
$$
	o_{i} = \sum_{j=1}^{n_{\text{in}}} w_{ji}x_{j}
$$
The weights $w_{ji}$ are all **drawn independently** from the **same distribution**.
Let us assume that this distribution has **zero mean** and **variance** $\sigma^2$.
Furthermore, we also assume that the inputs $x_{j}$ **also have zero mean** and **variance** $\gamma^2$, and that they are **independent** of $w_{ji}$ and **of each other**.
In this case, we can compute the *mean and variance* of $o_{i}$ as follows:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbb{E}[o_{i}] &= \sum_{j=1}^{n_{in}} \mathbb{E}[w_{ji}x_{j}] \\ \\
		&= \sum_{j=1}^{n_{in}} \mathbb{E}[w_{ji}] \mathbb{E}[x_{j}] \\ \\
		&= 0 \\ \\
		\text{Var}[o_{i}] &= \mathbb{E}[o_{i}^2] - \mathbb{E}^2[o_{i}] \\ \\
		&= \sum_{j=1}^{n_{\text{in}}} \mathbb{E}[w_{ji}^2 x_{j}^2] -0 \\ \\
		&= \sum_{j=1}^{n_{\text{in}}} \mathbb{E}[w_{ji}^2] \mathbb{E}[x_{j}^2] \\ \\
		&= n_{\text{in}}\sigma^2\gamma^2
	\end{align*}
$$
One way to **keep the variance fixed** (**signals** don't vanish or explode) is to set:
$$
	n_{\text{in}}\sigma^2 = 1
$$
Let us now **consider backpropagation**.
We face a similar problem, with the only difference being the fact that **propagation happens from the output to input layer**.
Thus, with the same reasoning we see that the **gradient**'s variance **blows up unless** we set:
$$
	n_{\text{out}}\sigma^2 = 1
$$
Where $n_{\text{out}}$ is the *number of outputs of this layer* (number of neurons in the considered layer).

Clearly, this leaves us in a dilemma: **we cannot possibly satisfy both conditions simultaneously**.
Instead, we **simply try to satisfy**:
$$
	\frac{1}{2}(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})\sigma^2 = 1
$$
Which is **equivalent to**:
$$
	\sigma = \sqrt{ \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}} }
$$
>[!def] XAVIER INITIALISATION
>When **weights** are **initialised by randomly drawing** them from a **distribution with the above** presented **variance**, we talk about **Xavier initialisation**.

Typically, Xavier initialisation samples weights from a *Gaussian distribution* with *zero mean* and variance:
$$
	\sigma^2 = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}}
$$
Notice that this is just **one possible choice**: the distribution doesn't necessarily have to be Gaussian, it just needs to have the **correct mean and variance**.

Thus, we could also adapt Xavier's intuition to **choose the right variance** if we want to **sample weights from a uniform distribution**.
Note that the uniform distribution $U(-a,a)$ has variance $a^2/3$. Plugging this into our condition for $\sigma^2$ yields the **value of** $a$ for the **correct interval**:
$$
	U\left( -\sqrt{ \frac{6}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}} }, +\sqrt{ \frac{6}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}} } \right)
$$
>[!note] CLARIFICATION
>Remember that $n_{\text{in}}$ and $n_{\text{out}}$ are **different for each layer**, they are **not the network's number of input and output neurons**.

Notice that, though the assumption for nonexistence of nonlinearities in the above reasoning can easily be violated in neural networks, the proposed method turns out to **work well in practice**.
## BATCH NORMALISATION
For a *deep neural network*, as we train, the **variables** (e.g. affine transformation outputs in an MLP) in **intermediate layers** may take values with **widely varying magnitudes**, both along the *layers from the input to the output* and *across units in the same layer*, as well as *over time* due to *our updates to the model parameters*.

It's been **informally postulated** that this drift in the distribution of such variables could **hamper the convergence** of the network.
Intuitively, one might conjecture that if one layer has variable values that are 100 times that of another layer, this might **necessitate compensatory adjustments in the learning rates**.

**Batch normalisation** is a form of *regularisation* that addresses this issue by **normalising the inputs** in **each training iteration** and applying a *scale coefficient* and a *scale offset*.
The normalisation relies on the mean and variance of the current minibatch, hence the name.
Formally, denoting $\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}$ an input of the current batch, batch normalisation tranforms $\mathbf{x}$ according to the following expression:
$$
	\text{BN}(\mathbf{x}) = \gamma \odot \frac{\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mu}_{\mathcal{B}}}{\hat{\sigma}_{\mathcal{B}}} + \beta 
$$
Where $\hat{\mu}_{\mathcal{B}}$ is the sample mean and $\hat{\sigma}_{\mathcal{B}}$ is the sample standard deviation of the minibatch $\mathcal{B}$; and $\gamma,\beta$ are the **scale coefficient and offset** (respectively), with the *same shape* as $\mathbf{x}$.
Notice that $\gamma$ and $\beta$ need to be **learnt jointly with the other model parameters**.

Formally, the mean and variance are computed as follows:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\hat{\mu}_{\mathcal{B}} &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|} \sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}} \mathbf{x} \\ \\
		\hat{\sigma}_{\mathcal{B}}^2 &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|} \sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}} (\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mu}_{\mathcal{B}})^2 + \epsilon
	\end{align*}
$$
Notice that we add a small constant $\epsilon$ to the variance estimate to *ensure we never attempt division by zero*.

Because these two parameters are **estimated on the minibatch** and **not the entire data set**, they **introduce noise**. One might think that this noisiness should be a problem, but as it turns out, this is **actually beneficial**.
This turns out to be a *recurring theme* in deep learning. For reasons that are **not yet well-characterised theoretically**, various sources of **noise** in optimisation lead to **faster training** and **less overfitting**.

>[!note] ON THE UTILITY OF NOISE
>Recall that we **do not** even **know why simpler DNNs generalise well** in the first place.
>Even with **dropout**, **weight decay** and **regularisation techniques**, they remain **so flexible** that their ability to generalise to unseen data **cannot be explained** via conventional approaches to learning theory.

Going back to the actual application of batch normalisation, we've only vaguely talked about **"layer variables"**, without specifying what we mean by that.
This is because batch normalisation can be **applied at different stages of computation** within layers:

>[!note] WHERE TO APPLY BATCH NORMALISATION
>When applying batch normalisation, the authors of the original paper performed it **after the affine transformations** and **before the activation function**, but later applications sometimes *insert batch normalisation* **right after the activation function**.

>[!idea] BATCH NORMALISATION IN PREDICTION MODE
>Once *training is complete*, why would we want the *same image* to be *classified differently depening on the batch* it happens to reside?
>Thus, once the model is trained, we **calculate the means and variances** of each layer's variables based on the **entire data set**.
## FUNCTION CLASSES
As we design increasingly deeper networks it becomes imperative to understand how **adding layers can increase** the complexity and **expressiveness of the network**.
Even more important is the ability to design networks where adding layers makes network **strictly more expressive** rather than *just different*.
This is where **function classes** come into play.

Consider $\mathcal{F}$, the **class of functions** that a specific network architecture **can reach**.
That is, for all $f\in\mathcal{F}$ there **exists some set of parameters** (e.g. weights and biases) that can be **obtained** through **training** on a suitable dataset.

Let us assume that $f^*$ is the **"truth" function** that we really would like to find. If it is in $\mathcal{F}$ we are in good shape, but typically we are not quite so lucky. 
Instead, we will try to find some $f^*_{\mathcal{F}}$ which is our **best guess** within $\mathcal{F}$. For instance, given a dataset with features $\mathbf{X}$ and labels $\mathbf{y}$, we might try finding it by solving the following optimisation problem:
$$
	f^*_{\mathcal{F}} := \underset{ f }{ \text{argmin} } L(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y},f) \text{ , }f\in\mathcal{F}
$$

>[!idea] FIRST INTUITION
>It **sounds reasonable** to assume that, if we design a different and **more powerful architecture** $\mathcal{F}'$ we should **arrive at a better outcome**; that is, we would expect that $f^*_{\mathcal{F}'}$ is "better" than $f^*_{\mathcal{F}}$. 
>
>However, if $\mathcal{F}\nsubseteq\mathcal{F}'$ there is **no guarantee that this should happen**.

In fact, $f^*_{\mathcal{F}'}$ **might even be worse**, as visually illustrated by the image below:

![[12_04_FunctionClasses.png]]

The figure shows us that, for **non-nested function classes**, a **larger function class** does **not always guarantee a better guess** of the "truth" function $f^*$.
For instance, on the left, $\mathcal{F}_{3}$ is closer to $f^*$ than $\mathcal{F}_{1}$, but $\mathcal{F}_{6}$ *moves away* and there is no guarantee that further increasing the complexity can reduce the distance from $f^*$.
The right part of the figure shows that this *doesn't happen with nested function classes*.

>[!note] RESIDUAL NETWORK
>Function classes were at the heart of the problems that He et al. were considering when working on *very deep computer vision models* for which they developed their **residual network**. See > [[2.0 - CONVOLUTIONAL NEURAL NETWORKS#RESNET]] for more details.
# ACTIVATION FUNCTIONS
In MLP networks, the (historically) most used activation function is the *sigmoid*. 
In deep neural networks, the use of this activation function is **problematic** for the **backpropagation** algorithm because it introduces the **vanishing gradient problem** (see > [[#EXPLODING/VANISHING GRADIENT PROBLEM]]).
This arises from the fact that the sigmoid has **saturating regions** at the **two ends of its domain**, where its **derivative is practically zero**:

>[!note] NOTE
>The **choice** of the **activation function** has **not yet been standardised** (officially) and is **still a widely discussed topic** in research papers.
>Here, we present some of the functions that appear in the literature more often, but keep in mind that **many more exist**.
## ReLU
>[!def] ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit)
>This function is defined as:
>$$ f(x) = \text{max}(0,x) $$

```tikz

```

Notice that the function is *not differentiable* at $x=0$, though for practical reasons its derivative is usually defined as follows:
$$
	f'(x) = \begin{cases}
	0 & x < 0 \\
	1 & x \geq 0
	\end{cases}
$$

>[!note] COMPARISON WITH THE SIGMOID
>There is **no saturation for positive values** since the **derivative** is **constant for positive values**.
>Furthermore, because the **derivative is null** for negative values, ReLU leads to **sparse activations** (some of the neurons are switched off), which have been observed to provide **robustness** to the network. 
## LEAKY ReLU, ELU
The derivative being null for all negative values **isn't always beneficial**: thus, ReLU suffers from the **dying ReLU problem**.

**ELU** and **Leaky ReLU** were both developed to solve this problem.

>[!def] ELU (Exponential Linear Unit)
>$$
>	f(x) = \begin{cases}
>	a(e^{ x }-1) & x < 0 \\
>	x & x \geq 0
>	\end{cases}
>$$
>With $a\in\mathbb{R}$.


Thus the derivative is given by:
$$
	f'(x) = \begin{cases}
	ae^{ x } & x < 0 \\
	1 & x \geq 0
	\end{cases}
$$
Notice that we still have $ae^{ x }\to 0$ when $x\to-\infty$, which means **ELU weakly solves the dying ReLU problem**.
A more robust solution is provided by ReLU.

>[!def] Leaky ReLU
>$$
>	f(x) = \begin{cases}
>	ax & x < 0 \\
>	x & x \geq 0
>	\end{cases}
>$$
>Where $a\in \mathbb{R}$ is usually **very small** (e.g. $0.01$).

Thus the derivativa is given by:
$$
	f'(x) = \begin{cases}
	a & x < 0 \\
	1 & x \geq 0
	\end{cases}
$$

>[!note] PReLU
>In some networks, the parameter $a$ in Leaky ReLU **can be learned** by the model.
>We refer to such implementations as **Parametric ReLU** (*PReLU*), denoted by $\text{PReLU}^{a}$. 
## SReLU
**S-Shaped ReLU** is composed of **three piece-wise linear functions** expressed by **four learnable parameters:

>[!def] SReLU
>$$
>	f(x) = \begin{cases}
>	t_{i}^r + a_{i}^r(x-t_{i}^r) & x \geq t_{i}^r \\
>	x & t_{i}^l < x < t_{i}^r \\
>	t_{i}^l + a_{i}^l(x-t_{i}^l) & x \leq t_{i}^l
>	\end{cases}
>$$
>Notice the *subscript* $i$ on the parameters: this notation indicates that there is **no fixed rule on the number of activation function parameters in the network**. 
>In other words, each neuron can potentially have its **own unique parameters**.

Below, we can see the resulting function for $t^r=0.4$, $a^r=0.2$, $t^l=-0.4$ and $a^l=0.2$.

The derivative is simply:
$$
	f'(x) = \begin{cases}
	a_{i}^r & x_{i} \geq t_{i}^r \\
	1 & t_{i}^l < x < t_{i}^r \\
	a_{i}^l & x \leq t_{i}^l
	\end{cases}
$$
The **learnable parameters** are $t^r,t^l,a^r,a^l$, usually initialised as follows:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		a^l = 0 & & t^l = 0 & & a^r = 1 & & a^l = \text{maxInput}
	\end{align*}
$$
Where $\text{maxInput}$ is a **hyperparameter**.
Notice that the **partial derivatives** of SReLU with respect to the *learnable parameters* are:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\frac{\partial f}{\partial t_{i}^r} &= I\{ x \geq t_{i}^r \}(1-a_{i}^r) \\ \\
		\frac{\partial f}{\partial a_{i}^r} &= I\{ x \geq t_{i}^r \}(x-t_{i}^r) \\ \\
		\frac{\partial f}{\partial t_{i}^l} &= I\{ x \leq t_{i}^l \}(1-a_{i}^l) \\ \\
		\frac{\partial f}{\partial a_{i}^l} &= I\{ x \leq t_{i}^l \}(x-t_{i}^l)
	\end{align*}
$$
These are needed for **backpropagation**. Since we added *more learnable paramaters*, we also need to compute:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\frac{\partial J}{\partial t_{i}^r} & & \frac{\partial J}{\partial a_{i}^r} \\ \\
		\frac{\partial J}{\partial t_{i}^l} & & \frac{\partial J}{\partial a_{i}^l}
	\end{align*}
$$

>[!idea] OBSERVATION
>SReLU's rather large **number of parameters** results in **high representation power**.
## APLU
**Adaptive Piecewise Linear Unit** combines ReLU with a **varying number of linear pieces**.
It is defined as follows:

>[!def] APLU
>$$ f(x) = \text{ReLU}(x) + \sum_{c=1}^n a_{c}\text{max}(0, b_{c}-x) $$
>Where $n$ is a **hyperparameter**, while the $a_c$ coefficients are **learned during training**.

>[!idea] OBSERVATION
>Notice that $b_c$ controls the **position of the "hinges".

>[!note] REGULARISATION
>During training, these parameters **are accounted for by regularisation** methods, but with a **penalty**, usually set to $0.001$ (this means the corresponding $J_{\text{reg,act}}$ term is multiplied by $0.001$).
>This is because, without the penalty, the optimiser would be *free to choose very large values* of $a_c$ and $b_c$ balanced by *very small weights*, which would lead to **numerical instability**.
## MEXICAN ReLU
First, we need to introduce a **"Mexican hat"-shaped function**:

>[!def] MEXICAN HAT FUNCTION
>$$ \phi^{a,\lambda}(x) = \text{max}(0,\lambda-|x-a|) $$
>Where $a,\lambda$ are real numbers.

These functions serve as the building blocks for **MeLU**, which is defined as follows:

>[!def] MEXICAN ReLU (MeLU)
>$$ f(x) = \text{MeLU}(x) = \text{PReLU}^{c_{0}}(x) + \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} c_{j}\phi^{a_{j},\lambda_{j}}(x) $$
>Where $k$ is the **number of learnable parameters** for each channel ($c_0$ for PReLU, $k-1$ for the Mexican hat functions: the $c_j$ coefficients), and $a_j,\lambda_j$ are **fixed and chosen recursively** starting from a $\text{maxInput}$ parameter.
