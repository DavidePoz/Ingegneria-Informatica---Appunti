# LEARNING RATE
When discussing *gradient descent* we've seen how the **update rule for weights** is:
$$
	\mathbf{w}_{\text{curr}} = \mathbf{w}_{\text{curr}} - \eta \nabla J(\mathbf{w}_{\text{curr}})
$$
And we said that $\eta$ is the **learning rate**.
**Calibrating** the learning rate is crucial: if it's *too low*, **convergence is too slow**; on the contrary, if it's *too large*, the weights **swing excessively** and may even **diverge**.

![[11_00_LearningRateCalibration.png]]

Notice that the **optimal value** depends on the **network architecture**, the choice of **loss function** and other parameters.
When working with *well known architectures* that have already been documented by others, it's usually recommended to **start with the suggested values** and then **slightly tweak them** by *monitoring convergence and generalisation*.

![[11_01_LearningRateCalibration2.png]]

>[!note] KEY NOTE
>During the experiment, we should **observe all three phases**, as this makes **choosing the optimal range trivial** (see figure above).
## LEARNING RATE ANNEALING
A commonly employed technique to **tune the value** of the learning rate is **learning rate annealing**.

>[!tldr] LEARNING RATE ANNEALING
>**Start** with a **high learning rate** and then **gradually lower it** during training.

The intuition behind this approach is that we would like to **traverse quickly** from the **initial parameters** to a **range of good parameter** values, but then we want a learning rate **small enough** to **explore** the **deeper but narrower parts** of the loss function.

The most popular form of learning rate annealing is a **step decay** where the learning rate is **reduced by some percentage after a set number of training epochs**.

![[11_02_LR_Annealing.png]]
## CYCLIC LEARNING RATE
More generally, we can establish that is useful to define a **learning rate schedule** according to which the learning rate is updated during training with some **specified rule**.

>[!tldr] CLR
>One possible solution is **cyclic learning rate**, which consists in changing the learning rate in a **cyclical way**, between **two bounds**.

Thus, it is important to define two things:
1. The **bounds** between which the LR varies.
2. The **step size**, that is, how many epochs will it take to *go from bound to another*.

![[11_03_CLR1.png]]

>[!idea] INTUITION
>A network with **good generalisation properties** should be robust in the sense that **small changes to the parameters shouldn't cause drastic changes in performance**. 
>Thus, we want to **avoid sharp local minima** and, by allowing our **learning rate** to **increase periodically**, we achieve exactly this:  *we jump out of these minima*, temporarily increasing the loss, but ultimately **leading to convergence on a more desirable minimum**.

Notice that, other than the triangular profile presented above, other forms of CLR are possible:

![[11_04_CLR2.png]]

![[11_05_CLR3.png]]

In the first case, `max_lr` is **halved after every cycle**, while in the second case it is **reduced exponentially** with each iteration.

There are **no mathematical rules** to set `max_lr` and `min_lr`. The suggested method is to **check past papers** related to the problem that is being analysed.

In any case, the LR schedule can be written as follows:
$$
	\eta_{t} = \eta_{\text{min}} + (\eta_{\text{max}}-\eta_{\text{min}})(\text{max}(0, 1-x))
$$
With the following parameters:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		x &= \left| \frac{i}{s} -2c + 1 \right| \\ \\
		c &= \left\lfloor  1 + \frac{i}{2s}  \right\rfloor 
	\end{align*}
$$
Where by $i$ is the current **iteration** (that is, how many *times we have performed a weight update*) and $s$ is the **step size**, or half the cycle length.

>[!note] NOTE
>Notice that $\eta_{max}$ can be a **decreasing function**, as seen in the figures above.

>[!idea] OBSERVATION
>Notice that $c$ is counting **which cycle we are in**. 
>The quantity $\frac{i}{s}-2c$ counts **how many half cycles are left in the current cycle** (thus, this value is in $[-2,0]$ and adding $1$ shifts it to $[-1,1]$).
>Finally, by applying the **absolute value** we obtain a **triangular shape for each cycle**.
>We consider $1-x$ so that the **schedule starts at the minimum value** and we multiply by $\eta_{\text{max}}-\eta_{\text{min}}$ to **match the desired interval**.

Furthermore, there exist **other cyclic patterns** that don't rely on triangular shapes.
Still, they are computed in very similar ways: below, **cosine annealing with warm restarts** is presented:
$$
	\eta_{t} = \eta_{\text{min}} + \frac{1}{2}(\eta_{\text{max}}-\eta_{\text{min}})\left(  1 + \cos\left(  \frac{T_{\text{curr}}}{T_{i}}\pi  \right)  \right)
$$
Where $T_{i}$ is the **number of epochs per cycle** and $T_{\text{curr}}$ is the **number of epochs since the last restart**. The profile ends up looking as shown below:

![[11_06_CLR4.png]]
## MOMENTUM
We've seen that SGD with **mini-batches** is a **compromise between noise and stability**, but it still causes a **"zig-zag" movement** of the gradient, which **slows convergence down** and makes the process **less stable**.

![[11_07_SGD_Comparisons.png]]

To address this problem, we can **add momentum to the delta value** applied to the **weights**: this added term **depends on the previous update** and has the effect of **preserving part of the previous "motion"**, thus **stabilising the path taken by the gradient** (just like momentum in physics).

![[11_08_Momentum.png]]

When momentum is considered, weight updates are calculated as follows:
$$
	\Delta w_{ij}^k = \eta \frac{\partial J}{\partial w_{ij}} + \underbrace{ \gamma \Delta w_{ij}^{k-1} }_{ \text{momentum} }
$$
Where $\Delta w_{ij}^{k-1}$ is the **previous weight update**.

![[11_09_SGDWithMomentum.png]]

In the figure above we see SGD without momentum on the left, and **SGD with momentum** on the **right**: applying momentum *adds stability*.

## OTHER APPROACHES
In addition to momentum, there are **other rules** that can be used to **update the weights** in a way that **accelerates convergence** of the gradient descent algorithm.
Some examples are:
- *Nesterov Accelerate Gradient* (**NAG**).
- *Adaptive Gradient* (**Adagrad**).
- **Adadelta**.
- **RMSProp**.
- **Adam**.

**Adam** is considered to be the **state of the art**, but **doesn't always deliver** better results than the classic SGD + Momentum approach.

Adam is an **optimiser** (introduced in 2014) that computes **adaptive learning rates for each parameter** by combining momentum and other ideas.
More precisely, Adam defines **two moving averages** ($m_{t}$, the *first moment*, and $u_{t}$, the *second moment*) that are then used to **update the weights**:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		m_{t} &= \rho_{1}m_{t-1} + (1-\rho_{1})g_{t} \\ \\
		u_{t} &= \rho_{2}u_{t-1} + (1-\rho_{2})g^2_{t}
	\end{align*}
$$
Where $g_{t}$ is the value of $\nabla J$ at *step* $t$ and the square on $g_{t}$ is *component-wise*.
The coefficients $\rho_{1}$ and $\rho_{2}$ are **hyperparameters** representing the **decay rate** for the moments (usually set to $0.9$ and $0.999$ respectively).
The moments are initialised to 0:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		m_{0} = 0 & & u_{0} = 0
	\end{align*}
$$
In order to take into account the fact that **value of moving averages** will be **very small due to the zero initialisation**, the authors of Adam defined **bias-corrected versions**:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\hat{m}_{t} &= \frac{m_{t}}{1-\rho_{1}} \\ \\
		\hat{u}_{t} &= \frac{u_{t}}{1-\rho_{2}}
	\end{align*}
$$
Finally, the **update for each weight** of the network is computed as follows:
$$
	w_{ij}^t = w_{ij}^{t-1} -\eta \frac{\hat{m}_{t}}{\sqrt{ \hat{u}_{m} }+\varepsilon}
$$
Where $\eta$ is the learning rate and $\varepsilon$ is a **very small positive number** to **prevent divisions by zero**, usually set to $10^{ -8 }$.
# OVERFITTING AND DROPOUT
In > [[0.3 - STATISTICS REVISION#BIAS-VARIANCE TRADEOFF]] we discussed a key problem in machine learning known as **bias-variance trade-off**.

In particular, **linear models** show **high bias** because they *can only represent a small class of functions*. However, these models also have **low variance**: they give similar results across different random samples of the data.

On the contrary, **deep neural networks** inhabit the *opposite end* of the *bias-variance* spectrum. Because they are *much more complex*, they are **not confined to looking at each feature individually** and can instead **learn interactions among groups of features**.
This translates to an interesting fact:

>[!note] BIAS-VARIANCE CONSEQUENCE
>**Linear** models **overfit** when presented with **more features than examples**.
>Conversely, **deep neural networks** tend to **overfit** when faced with **more examples than features**.

In 2017, a group of researchers demonstrated the extreme flexibility of these networks by training DNNs on **randomly-labeled images**.
Despite the **absence of a true pattern** linking the inputs to the outputs, they found that the neural network optimised by SGD could **label every image in the training set perfectly**.

Notice that this means that if **labels are assigned uniformly at random** and there are, say, 10 classes, then **no classifier can do better than 10% accuracy** on *holdout data*.

This raises the question:

>[!idea] QUESTION
>If these models are **so complex** and so expressive that they **can overfit this badly**, then **when should we expect them not to overfit?**

The mathematical foundations for the puzzling generalisation properties of deep neural networks remain open research questions.
## DROPOUT
The **key expectation** we have from a **good predictive model** is **good performance on unseen data**.
Classical generalisation theory suggests that, to close the gap between train and test performance, we should aim for a **simple model**.

Simplicity may come in the form of a **small number of dimensions**; but another useful notion of simplicity is **smoothness**, that is, the function should **not be sensitive to small changes in its inputs**.
For instance, when classifying images, we would expect that **adding random noise to the pixels should be harmless**.

In 1995, *Christopher Bishop* formalised this idea when he proved that *training input noise is equivalent to Tikhonov regularisation*. This work drew a clear mathematical connection between the requirement that a function be **smooth** and the requirement that it be **resilient to perturbations** in the input.

A clever idea to apply this idea to the internal layers of a network was developed by *Srivastava et al.* in 2014.
Namely, they proposed to **inject noise onto each layer** of the network in the *forward propagation step* during training and observed that this **enforces smoothness just on the input-output mapping**.

This idea is known as **dropout**:

>[!def] DROPOUT
>Throughout **training**, on *each iteration*, dropout consists of **zeroing out some fraction of the nodes** in **each layer** before calculating the subsequent layer.

Note that dropout falls in the category of **regularisation techniques**, among the ones presented in > [[1.0 - NEURAL NETWORKS INTRODUCTION#LOSS REGULARISATION]] and others:

![[11_12_DropoutRegularisation.png]]

The key challenge then is **how to inject this noise in an unbiased manner** so that the **expected value of each layer**, while fixing the others, **remains equal** to the value it would have **outputted without noise**.

In standard dropout regularisation, one "debiases" each layer by **normalising** by the **fraction of nodes that were retained** (not dropped out).
In other words, with *dropout probability* $p$, each intermediate **activation** $h$ is **replaced by a random variable** $h'$ defined as follows:
$$
	h' = \begin{cases}
	0 & \text{with probability } p \\ \\
	\displaystyle\frac{h}{1-p} & \text{otherwise}
	\end{cases}
$$
Thus, we have:
$$
	\mathbb{E}[h'] = p\cdot0 + (1-p) \frac{h}{1-p} = h
$$
Just as we wanted.

![[11_13_Dropout.png]]

>[!idea] DROPOUT VISUALISATION
>Notice that when *zeroing out each unit* with *probability* $p$, the **result** can be viewed as a **network containing only a subset of the original neurons**.
>In the example figure above, $h_2$ and $h_5$ are removed, and their **respective gradient vanishes during backpropagation**.
>This way, the calculation of the **output layer cannot be overly dependent** on **any one element** of $h_1,\dots,h_5$. 

We can imagine that **if neurons are randomly dropped out** of the network during training, then **other neurons will have to step in** and handle the representation required to **make predictions for the missing neurons**. 
This is believed to result in **multiple independent internal representations** being learned by the network: the effect is that the network becomes **less sensitive to the specific weights** of neurons. 
This in turn results in a network that is capable of **better generalisation** and is **less likely to overfit** the training data.

>[!note] NOTE
>Typically, **dropout is disabled at test time**.
>However, there are some **exceptions**: researchers may use dropout at test time as **heuristic for estimating** the **uncertainty of a network** predictions.
>In fact, **if predictions are agree** across many **different dropout masks**, then we might say that the **network is more confident**.
# DATA AUGMENTATION
To help **reduce chances of overfitting** when training a machine learning model, we can employ **data augmentation techniques**.

>[!def] DATA AUGMENTATION
>Data augmentation techniques are used to **increase the amount of data** by **adding slightly modified copies of already existing training patterns** or **creating "synthetic" data** from them.

>[!note] NOTE
>Notice that data augmentation techniques are **problem-specific**: for instance, standard approaches used in *computer vision* for image augmentation *deteriorate the performance in audio classification*.

Since *spectrograms* have *time and frequency* on their axes, standard image augmentation techniques, e.g. reflection, will **hardly make sense**. 
Consider this example: if a *bird's hum* is characterised by an increasing pitch over the its duration, a reflection would represent a *completely different sound* where the pitch decreases over its duration.

Here, we will be mainly discussing **image augmentation techniques**, since image classification is amongst the *harder classification problems*.
Keep in mind that the motivations behind the need for data augmentation apply to all contexts, but remember the note above and *do not employ these methods to other domains*.
## MOTIVATION, INTRA-CLASS VARIATIONS AND GENERALISATION
Deep neural networks must be **invariant** to a **wide variety of inputs**.
That is, we want our model to **not be affected by small variations in the inputs**.

Take the following images as an example:

![[11_14_Otters.png]]

Imagine we are training a model to **recognise animals**: all the images above are pictures of *otters*, but they **vary in the animal's position**. 
These variations are known as *intra-class variations*:

>[!def] INTRA-CLASS VARIATIONS
>Small **variations** in the **data within a certain class**.
>

Notice that other kinds of *intra-class variations* that exist in images are appearance, lighting, orientation etc.
This **large amount** of variations makes **image classification particularly challenging**.

To train a model that **can recognise these variations** we need to **train it more data**, but this leads to a practical problem: **data is often limited**.

Thus, the goal of data augmentation is the following:

>[!idea] MOTIVATION
>Create **"fake"** data from the *available existing patterns* and **add it to the training set** to **achieve the desired variation** in **training data**.

Notice that this process is **often "on the fly"**: augmented images are **generated using random transformations** from each image in the batch at the beginning of every iteration, but they **are not stored**.

It's also crucial to guarantee the **preservation of semantics**; that is, augmented data **should not change the class** of the original pattern.
## GEOMETRIC TRANSFORMATIONS
Even **simple geometric operations** often **greatly improve generalisation**.
Some examples are:

>[!tldr] IMAGE CROPPING
>- **Randomly crop** and *re-scale* images.
>- Do **not crop small regions**, as *semantics could be lost*.

>[!tldr] IMAGE FLIPPING
>- **Flip** the original images **horizontally** with **some given probability** $p$.
>- **Flip** the original images **vertically** with some **other probability** $p'$.
>
>**NOTE** This can't be naively applied to all contexts. Think of *street-related images*: horizontal flipping can **alter the notions of traffic lanes**, leading to *semantic loss*.

>[!tldr] AFFINE TRANSFORMATION
>- Apply a **single affine/linear transformation** to the 2D image space (i.e. multiply the image matrix by the transformation matrix. Examples are scaling, rotation, shear.).
>- Fill **blank spaces** with strategies like *constant colour*, *expand colours*, *replicate colours*.

![[11_15_AffineTrans.png]]

>[!tldr] PIECEWISE AFFINE TRANSFORMATION
>- Similar to *affine transformation*, though now a **single transformation** is **not applied to the whole image**.
>- The image is perceived as an underlying **grid**, where each grid undergoes an **independent transformation**.

![[11_16_PiecewiseAffine.png]]

>[!tldr] PERSPECTIVE TRANSFORMATION
>- Transformations with **more degrees of freedom**.
>- The perspective effect causes **certain regions** to be **enlarged** and others to be **squeezed**.

![[11_17_Perspective.png]]
## LOCAL FILTERS
Other commonly employed transformations rely on image **blurring/sharpening** and **edge detection**.

>[!tldr] GAUSSIAN BLUR
>- Applies a **normally distributed blur filter** onto the given image with a $\sigma$ chosen at random from a pre-specified interval.
>- Helps the model to **recognise objects at different resolutions**, making it more robust to *camera-induced* variations in captured images.

![[11_18_Blur.png]]

>[!tldr] IMAGE SHARPENING
>- Does the **opposite of introducing blur**. The resulting effect is a **sharper image**.

>[!tldr] EDGE DETECTION
>- Uses the **edge-detected** version of a given image.
>- It's important to verify that the **detected edges do not change the class** of the objects.

![[11_19_EdgeDetection.png]]
## ADDING NOISE
It's been observed that deep neural networks are **highly sensitive to noisy images**.
Thus, it very likely that *adding even a small amount of noise in an image* would lead to *drastic reductions in performance*, even though the *human eye would be mostly undisturbed*.

**Adding noise** is therefore **crucial to help the model generalise better**.
The most commonly used technique is **Gaussian noise**:

>[!tldr] GAUSSIAN NOISE
>Add a fixed **Gaussian noise** (this simply means *adding normally distributed values*) to **each pixel in the image**.
>The additive noise is **chosen randomly** for **different images** in the data set.

![[11_20_GaussianNoise.png]]

>[!tldr] SALT AND PEPPER NOISE
>Each **pixel** in the image is **can be turned either black or white** depending on a **specific probability**.

![[11_21_SaltPepperNoise.png]]

**Colour transformations** are also extremely useful, as cameras produce different **colour spectra** depending on the **type of sensors** used, as well as the *white-balancing* performed.

In any case, the *main reason* these transformations are applied is to help the model **generalise to different lighting conditions**. In fact, the captured colours of a scene vary dramatically **depending on the time of the day**.

## COMBINING TECHNIQUES
In practice, **all the techniques above are combined** and then **randomly applied** on the fly to **different images drawn at random** from within the mini-batch (e.g. we can *rotate an image* and *then apply the fog filter*).

>[!note] IMPORTANT REMARKS
>- When **comparing two networks** we **must make sure to use the same augmentation techniques** for **both**, as data augmentation can *significantly improve performance* and will certainly drive us to *wrong conclusions* if different augmentation strategies are employed for the two models being compared.
>- Data augmentation must be considered **part of the network design**.
>- It is important to specify the **right distribution of transformations**: not all of them may be useful to a particular task, therefore the **helpful ones** and **their strength** are **hyperparameters** that must be **chosen carefully**.
