# DATA AND PATTERNS
Data is a **fundamental ingredient of machine learning**, where the behaviour of the algorithms is *not pre-programmed*, but **learned from the data** itself. 
Terms like Data Science, Data Mining, Big Data emphasise the role of data. 
We will often use the term **"pattern"** to refer to data.

>[!def] PATTERN (Def. by S. Watanabe)
>A *pattern* can be defined as **the opposite of chaos**.
>>*A vaguely defined entity that can be given a name*.

For example, a pattern can be a *face*, a *handwritten character*, a *fingerprint*, a *sound signal*, a *fragment of text*, the *performance of a stock exchange*, and so on.

>[!def] PATTERN RECOGNITION
>The discipline that *deals with the recognition of patterns*.
>
>**NOTE**: Not necessarily with ML, it may also use *pre-programmed algorithms*.
## KINDS OF PATTERNS
There are **many kinds** of patterns, let's take a look at some of them.

>[!def] NUMERICAL PATTERNS
>Values associated with *measurable characteristics*.
>- They may be *continuous* or *discrete*, in both cases *subject to order*.
>- They are naturally **represented** as **numerical vectors** in *multidimensional space*.

>[!example] EXAMPLE OF NUMERICAL PATTERN
>Person: [height, chest circumference, hip circumference, foot length]

>[!def] CATEGORICAL PATTERNS
>Values associated with *qualitative characteristics* and the *presence/absence* of a feature (*yes/no* value).
>- They are not *semantically* mappable into numerical values.
>- They are normally managed by *rule systems* and *classification trees*.

>[!def] SEQUENTIAL PATTERNS
>Sequences with *spatial* or *temporal relationships*, often *variable in length*.
>- The *position in the sequence* and *relationships with predecessors and successors* are fundamental.
>- They can be thought of as numerical patterns where *components are spatially or temporally related*.

>[!example] EXAMPLES OF SEQUENTIAL PATTERNS
>Audio streams, text streams, proteins (sequences of amino acids).
# PATTERN REPRESENTATION IN BIOLOGY
As seen above, *proteins* are examples of *sequential patterns*, and so are *DNA fragments*. As a consequence, *pattern representation* finds many applications in *bioinformatics*, *drug development* and, in general, in all of *biology*.

Here, we present a few examples of such applications with the intent of building an *intuition* for developing pattern representation *strategies* in future cases.
## PROTEINS
>[!idea] REMINDER
>A **protein** is a *sequence of amino acids*. There exist *20 different amino acids* in nature.

![[AminoAcidsTable.png]]

A *naive* way to represent a protein of *length* $N$ would be using a *vector* $AS\in \mathbb{R}^N$, each component is given by:
$$
	AS(i) = \frac{h(i)}{N} \text{ , } i\in[1,\dots,20]
$$
Here, $h(i)$ is the *number of occurrences* of a given amino acid in the protein.
This solution is an example of **purely numerical** pattern, but we can do better: we know that the **position** of an amino acid in the protein is not random, it *carries a meaning* that we aren't *encoding* with the representation above.

We thus want to find a representation strategy that takes this extra information into account.
A first step in this direction could be assuming that one amino acid's *role* could be influenced by the *presence of another*: this means **counting the occurrences of each pair** of amino acids.
This representation is known as **2-Gram** (*2G*) and is defined as follows:
$$
	\text{2G}(k) = \left( \frac{h(i,j)}{N} \right) \text{ , }i,j\in[1,\dots,20]
$$
Here, $k=j+20(i-1)$ iterates over *all possible pairs*.

A more complex approach is given the **autocovariance (AC) representation**.
Given a parameter $m$ denoting the *maximum distance between two different amino acids* in a protein $P=(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{N})$ of *length* $N$, and a *physicochemical property* $d$, $\text{AC}^d(i)$ is defined as follows:
$$
	\text{AC}^d(i) = \begin{cases}
	h(i)/N & i\in[1,\dots,20] \\ \\
	\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N-i+20} \frac{(\text{index}(p_{k},d) - \mu_{d})(\text{index}(p_{k+i-20})-\mu_{d})}{\sigma_{d}(N-i+20)} & i\in[21,\dots,20+m]
	\end{cases}
$$
Therefore $\text{AC}^d\in \mathbb{R}^{20+m}$.
**NOTE**: The parameter $\mu_{d}$ is the *mean value* for property $d$ and $\sigma_{d}$ is the *variance* of the values for property $d$.

>[!note] OBSERVATION
>The first 20 components of the *feature vector* are simply the *frequencies of each amino acid*.

To better understand the last $m$ components introduced by this strategy, let's take a look at the following table:

![[AC_Approach_ExTable.png]]
In the above example we have *5 properties* (polarity, secondary structure, ...), therefore $d=1,\dots,5$.

>[!example] EXAMPLE EXPLANATION
>In this setting, we would have:
>- $\text{index}(A,1)=-0.591$
>- $\text{index}(C,3)=0.465$

Now let's define $\text{lag}:=i-20$; with $i\in[21,\dots,20+m]$ we have $\text{lag}\in[1,\dots,m]$.
Looking at the definition of the last $m$ components, we immediately notice that we are checking the **values** (or, more precisely, the distance from the mean) of property $d$ at **positions** $k$ and  $k+\text{lag}$.

>[!note] INTERPRETATION
>We are considering **pairs** of amino acids that are **separated by _lag_ other amino acids** and iterating for values of $\text{lag}$ ranging from $1$ (*minimum distance*) to $m$ (*maximum distance*).
>
>With this approach, we can detect *whether certain properties are influenced by other amino acids at set distances*. 
>This is important because these relations may emerge because of *interactions* caused by  the **protein's 3D structure** (proteins aren't straight, they are folded in complicated ways).
## DNA
The *frequencies of occurrence* can also be used for DNA sequences.
Let's consider a sequence of nucleotides $\text{Nuc}$:
$$
	\text{Nuc} = [R_{l}] \text{ , }l=1,2,3,\dots,L
$$
We can use both the *occurrence of the single nitrogenous base* (denoted $f(\text{NitBase})$), in which case our *feature vector* would be:
$$
	F = [f(A),f(C),f(G),f(T)]
$$
or the *occurrences of pairs* (denoted $f(\text{NitBase}_{1}\text{NitBase}_{2})$), in which case we would obtain:
$$
	F = [f(AA), f(AC), f(AG), f(AT), \dots, f(TT)]
$$
A more complex approach is given by the *pseudo nucleic acid representation*, which describes a DNA sequence using the following $g_{i}$ entries for the feature vector:
$$
	\begin{align*}
	g_{1} &= \frac{1}{L-2} \sum_{i=1}^{L-2} \Delta(R_{i}R_{i+1}, R_{i+1}R_{i+2}) \\ \\
	g_{2} &= \frac{1}{L-3} \sum_{i=1}^{L-3} \Delta(R_{i}R_{i+1}, R_{i+2}R_{i+3}) \\ \\
	g_{1} &= \frac{1}{L-4} \sum_{i=1}^{L-4} \Delta(R_{i}R_{i+1}, R_{i+3}R_{i+4}) \\ \\
	\dots \\ \\
	g_{\omega} &= \frac{1}{L-\omega-1} = \sum_{i=1}^{L-\omega-1} \Delta(R_{i}R_{i+1}, R_{i+\omega}R_{i+\omega+1})
	\end{align*}
$$
Where the terms in the summations are given by:
$$
	\Delta(R_{i}R_{i+1}, R_{k}R_{k+1}) = \frac{1}{J} \sum_{j=1}^J [V_{j}(R_{i}R_{i+1}) - V_{j}(R_{k}R_{k+1})]^2
$$
The parameter $J$ is the number of *properties* and $V_{j}(a,b)$ is the value of the $j$-th property for the base pair $ab$ (these values are gathered in tables, just like in the earlier example regarding proteins).
## DNA MICROARRAYS
A DNA **microarray** (also commonly known as *DNA chip or biochip*) is a **collection of microscopic DNA spots** attached to a solid surface. 
Scientists use DNA microarrays to *measure the expression levels of large numbers of genes simultaneously* or to genotype multiple regions of a genome. 
Each DNA spot contains picomoles (10−12 moles) of a specific DNA sequence, known as a **probe** (or **reporter** or **oligo**).

A typical use is to *compare the gene expression* profile of a *sick individual* with that of a *healthy one* to identify *which genes are involved in the disease*, or to choose the appropriate drug based on gene expression. 

The problem is that a microarray allows the expression of over 20,000 genes to be extracted, therefore each DNA sample is described by over 20,000 elements, **most of which are not related to the given classification process**.

Techniques called **features selection** are used to *select only some gene expressions*. 
E.g. *signal to noise* methods calculate, for each gene $j$ (example valid for a bi-classification problem: relevant/irrelevant), a coefficient given by:
$$
	S(j) = \frac{\mu_{+}(j) - \mu_{-}(j)}{\sigma_{+}(j) - \sigma_{-}(j)}
$$
>[!idea] APPROACH EXPLANATION
>The genes that give positive values are correlated with the class *+1*, while the genes that give negative values are correlated with the class *-1*. 
>At the end, $m/2$ positive and $m/2$ negative genes are selected.
# OTHER APPROACHES
We conclude this chapter by presenting 2 more powerful approaches in pattern representation.

The first one is known as **LBP** (*Local Binary Pattern*) a type of visual descriptor used for classification in computer vision, which deals with the analysis and classification of images by machines.

The second one is based on **dissimilarity spaces**, a kind of *vector spaces* that can be applied to problems in many different domains.
## LOCAL BINARY PATTERNS
LBP was first described in 1994 and it has since been found to be a powerful feature for **texture classification**.

Given an *image* divided into *windows*, the LBP feature vector, in its simplest form, is created in the following manner:

>[!tldr] OBTAINING THE LBP VECTOR
>1. **Divide** the examined window into *cells* (e.g. 16x16 pixels for *each cell*).
>2. For *each pixel in a cell* **compare** the pixel to its **8 neighbours** (top-left, top-middle, top-right, etc., following the pixels **along a circle** (i.e. clockwise or counter-clockwise).
>3. Where the **centre** pixel's value is **greater** than the neighbour value **write 0**; otherwise, write 1. This gives an **8-digit binary number**, which can be thought of as a vector of 8 components, or a decimal number in $[0,255]$ for convenience.
>4. **Compute** the histogram, **over the cell** (16x16 in the example we're considering), of the *frequency of each "number" occurring*. This histogram can be seen as a 256-dimensional feature vector.
>5. **Concatenate** (*normalized*) histograms of all cells to obtain a feature vector for the *entire window*.

The LBP operator was also extended to use *neighbourhoods of different sizes*. Using a *circular neighbourhood* and bilinearly *interpolating values at non-integer coordinates* allow *any radius* and *number of pixels* in the neighbourhood.

![[LBP_Pattern.png]]

The LBP value for a neighbourhood of $P$ points and radius $R$ (The neighbourhood is thus denoted by $(P,R)$) is given by:
$$
	\text{LBP}_{P,R} = \sum_{p=0}^{P-1}s(g_{p}-g_{C})2^p
$$
Where:
$$
	s(x) = \begin{cases}
	1 & x \geq 0 \\
	0 & x < 0
	\end{cases}
$$
And $g_{p}$ denotes the *grayscale value* of pixel $p$.

A useful *extension* to the original operator is the so-called **uniform pattern**, which can be used to **reduce the length of the feature vector** and implement a simple **rotation invariant descriptor**. 
This idea is motivated by the fact that **some binary patterns occur more commonly in texture images** than others. 

A local binary pattern is *called uniform* if the binary pattern *contains at most two 0-1 or 1-0 transitions*. For example, 00010000 (2 transitions) is a uniform pattern, but 01010100 (6 transitions) is not.

>[!idea] WHY UNIFORM PATTERNS ARE HELPFUL
>In the computation of the *LBP histogram*, the histogram has a **separate bin for every uniform pattern**, and all **non-uniform patterns are assigned to a single bin**. 
>
>Hence why it helps **reduce the dimension of the feature vector**: using uniform patterns with $(8,R)$ neighbourhoods, the length of the feature vector for a single cell *reduces from 256 to 59* (58 bins for all the possible uniform patters, and another bin for all the others).

It is important to note that *images carry spatial information*, therefore the following observation must be kept in mind.

>[!note] OBSERVATION
>The descriptors seen above, if *calculated over the entire image*, do **not retain spatial information**. 
>
>To **enrich** the descriptor with **local information** we **divide the image into regions** (or windows, as we called them earlier) and **calculate the descriptors for each region**.
>These regions may **overlap** or not, depending on the problem.
## DISSIMILARITY SPACES
Some patterns can be described by a series of numbers, where each number is the **"similarity"** between a given **"original pattern"** (a *reference*) and another one. 
This way, the reference **patterns can also be complex structures**, as long as we can come up with a way to "*measure differences between them*".

Examples of such structures are graphs, in which case a *series of distances* between graphs would be the representation vector of patterns.

The precise *mathematical tool* to deal with such cases is a **dissimilarity space**:

>[!def] DISSIMILARITY SPACE
>A **vector space** in which the dimensions are defined by **dissimilarity vectors** measuring **pairwise dissimilarities** *between examples and individual objects* from the so-called *representation set* $R$.
>

>[!note] NOTE
>The *representation set* can be chosen as the *complete training set*, or as a *set of carefully selected or constructed prototypes*.

Hence, a *dissimilarity representation* $D(X,R)$ is addressed as a data-dependent mapping
$$
	D(\cdot,R):X \to \mathbb{R}^n
$$
from an initial set of objects $X$ to a *dissimilarity space*, equipped with the traditional *inner product* and a *metric*.

To better understand this concept, consider the following explanation.
Assume a representation set composed of $n$ *reference objects* $R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{n}$, and assume that we have a set of objects $O=o_{1},o_{2},\dots$.
Then each $o_{i}\in O$ can be represented as a *dissimilarity vector* $D_{i}$:
$$
	D_{i} := [d(o_{i},R_{1}),d(o_{i},R_{2}),\dots,d(o_{i},R_{n})] \in \mathbb{R}^n
$$
Where $d(o_{j},R_{k})$ is a **metric** that outputs the *dissimilarity* between *object* $o_{j}$ and *reference* $R_{k}$.

>[!note] NOTE
>Note that:
>1. The metric $d$ *could* be as simple as outputting the *difference between two values*.
>2. More precisely, $d$ is a **pseudometric** (see explanation below).

>[!idea] METRICS AND PSEUDOMETRICS
>Remember that a **metric** $d$ over a *set* $M$ is a *function*
>$$ d:M\times M \to \mathbb{R} $$
>such that for any $x,y,z\in M$, the following hold:
>1. $d(x,y)=0 \Leftrightarrow x=y$
>2. $d(x,y)=d(y,x)$
>3. $d(x,z)\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z)$
>
>For a **pseudometric**, the first condition changes to:
>$$ d(x,x) = 0 $$
>That is, one may have $d(x,y)=0$ for distinct values $x\ne y$.
