# INTRODUCTION
In the previous chapter we covered the core machine learning topics that are key to the understanding of deep learning, but ML alone isn't a strong enough basis.

Since this field is deeply (pun intended) connected with *data analysis*, having familiarity with statistics concepts is not only recommended, but absolutely essential.

Therefore, this chapter aims at *revising known concepts* in probability and statistics, and introducing new ones that will then be used throughout the rest of the course.
## CLT REVISION
It's probably worth it to start this revision from the **Central Limit Theorem** (*CLT*), a crucial pillar of statistics and machine learning. 
The theorem is a key concept in probability theory because it implies that probabilistic and **statistical methods that work for normal distributions can be applicable to many problems involving other types of distributions**.

The statement is as follows:

>[!th] CENTRAL LIMIT THEOREM
>Let $(X_{i})_{i\geq 1}$ be a sequence of **independent and identically distributed** random variables with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$; and let $\bar{X}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}[X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}]$ be the **sample mean** of a sample of $n$ variables of the sequence.
>Then, as $n$ approaches infinity, the following is true:
>$$
>	\frac{\bar{X}_{n} - \mu}{\sigma /n} \underset{ n\to \infty }{ \longrightarrow } \mathcal{N}(0,1)
>$$

>[!note] IMPORTANT NOTE
>The usefulness of the theorem stems from the fact that it **holds even if the original variables** $X_{i}$ themselves **are not normally distributed**, greatly simplifying the analysis of random variables *for large sample sizes*.

Another crucial theorem in statistics is the (*strong*) **law of large numbers**:

>[!th] (STRONG) LAW OF LARGE NUMBERS
>$$ P\left(\lim_{ n \to \infty } \overline{X}_{n} = \mu\right) = 1 $$

The key takeaway for us is that if we take a *sufficiently large sample size* from a *population with finite variance*, the extracted **mean of all samples** $\bar{X}_{n}$ will be a **good approximation of the original population mean**.
# HYPOTHESIS TESTING AND P VALUE
Hypothesis testing is a **formal procedure** that involves the use of statistics to investigate our ideas about the world. 
Scientists most commonly use it to **test specific predictions** derived from theories, which we call hypotheses.

A key concept in hypothesis testing is that of the **null hypothesis**:

>[!def] NULL HYPOTHESIS
>A *Null Hypothesis*, denoted $\text{H0}$, expresses the **opposite of what a researcher predicts** or anticipates.

This definition may appear confusing at first, but the *general procedure* of hypothesis testing should make its role clear:
1. Define the **null hypothesis** based on the research question.
2. Set a **significance level**. This means choosing a *threshold* to determine the *risk of a false positive error*; that is, the risk of finding a result that *doesn't actually exist*.
3. Calculate the **test statistic**. This consists of computing a value from the sample data using an appropriate test.
4. Determine the **P value**. This is the *probability of observing the data if the null hypothesis were true*.
5. Make a **decision**: we *compare* the p-value with the *significance level* and decide whether we can *reject the null hypothesis* or not.

>[!idea] HYPOTHESIS TESTING IN SIMPLE TERMS
>We formulate $\text{H0}$ as the **opposite of what we observed**, then we compute the probability of obtaining a **result at least as extreme** as our observation **if H0 were true** via a *test statistic*.
>If the obtained **p-value is small enough** it means that if H0 were true, our observations would have been very unlikely and we can thus **reject H0 in favour of our own hypothesis**.

There exist many different **test statistics**, which can be grouped into two main categories:
1. **Parametric tests**: these *assume the data follows a specific distribution* and rely on *population parameters* such as the mean. They are mainly used for *continuous data* (intervals or ratios).
2. **Non parametric tests**: these *do not assume a specific data distribution*. They are applicable to a wider range of data types, including *nominal* and *ordinal*.

Here, we only present two of these tests. The rationale behind these two can then be applied to all the others.
## Z TEST
The **Z-Test** can be used to analyse *two populations* and decide whether their **means differ or not** when *variances are known* and the *sample size* is *large enough* (indicatively, we want 30 or more samples).
More precisely, when we use this test we are assessing whether the **observed sample mean is consistent with an hypothesised value of the population mean** under the null hypothesis. 

Usually, the *population mean* $\mu$ is hypothesised (e.g. from historical data) and the *population standard deviation* $\sigma$ is *assumed to be known*.

Under these assumptions, we compute the **z test statistic** as follows:
$$
	z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{ n }}
$$
Where $\bar{X}$ is the *observed sample mean* and $n$ is the *sample size*.

We then have to compare this $z$ value with a *critical value* that *depends on the desired significance level* and on whether the test is **one-tailed** or **two-tailed**.

>[!idea] ONE-TAILED VS TWO-TAILED TESTS
>- **ONE-TAILED TEST**: We believe that the *population mean* is *greater than* (in which case we call it a *right-tailed test*) or *lower than* (in which case we call it a *left-tailed test*) the *value stated in H0*.
>- **TWO-TAILED TEST**: We simply believe that *the population mean is different from the value stated in H0*, but we *do not care if it's greater or lower*.

The most commonly used critical values for the z-test are presented in the following table:

|            | 10%   | 5%    | 1%    |
| ---------- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| ONE-TAILED | 1.280 | 1.645 | 2.330 |
| TWO-TAILED | 1.645 | 1.960 | 2.580 |

To better understand how the test works, let's consider the following example.

>[!tldr] EXAMPLE
>We are told that the **mean score of students** in a class **is 70** with a **standard deviation of 10**.
>We select a **sample of 50 students** with a **mean score of 80**.
>Is there enough evidence to **reject** at a **5% significance level** that the mean score is **70**?

Let's formalise the hypotheses at play for clarity:
- **H0** (*null hypothesis*): the **mean score is 70**.
- **H1** (*our hypothesis*, formulated from the sampled students): the **mean score is greater than 70**.

>[!note] FORMALITIES ABOUT H0
>To be more precise, we should say:
>- **H0**: the true **(population) mean** and the **sample mean** are **the same**.
>- **H1**: the **two means are different** and, more precisely, the **sample mean is greater**.
>  
>This puts an emphasis on the fact that H0 is stating that *our observations are wrong* (after all, we said H0 is the *opposite* of what we're claiming), but perhaps the (equivalent) formulation presented above is *more intuitive*.

Since we are making an assumption about the "*direction*" of the hypothesis, this is a **one-tailed test**, specifically a *right-tailed test*.
We have $\bar{X}=80$, $\mu=70$, $\sigma=10$ and $n=50$.
Therefore, by computing $z$ with the above formula we obtain:
$$
	z = \frac{\bar{X}-\mu}{\sigma /\sqrt{ n }} = \frac{80-70}{10 /\sqrt{ 50 }} \approx 7.07
$$
Since we want a 5% confidence level, our *critical z value* $z^*$ is:
$$
	z^* = 1.960
$$

>[!idea] CONCLUSION
>We have:
>$$ z > z^* $$
>Therefore we **can reject the null hypothesis** in favour of our own: the two *means are not the same* and the *sample mean is greater*.

The *critical z values* are obtained from a *z value table* (see figure below), which contains the values of the *cumulative distribution function* of a **normal distribution** (remember that this is a function defined as $\text{CDF}(z)=P(Z<z)$ where $Z\sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$).

Note that for a 5% confidence level we have to look for a $z$ value corresponding to a 95% probability for a *one-tailed test* and to 97.5% for a *two-tailed test*. This is because in the first case we're interested in the 5% values at **just one end of the distribution**, while in the second case we're interested in the 5% values at **both ends**.

![[03_00_z_table.png]]

>[!idea] ALTERNATIVE Z TEST APPROACH
>We can also use this table to *decide if we can reject H0* by **determining the probability associated with our z value** and **comparing it with the significance level** requested.

In the above example we had $z=7.07$: note that this value is so extreme that it *doesn't show up in the table* (which only goes up to $3.99$).
It's thus reasonable to consider $F(z) = \text{CDF(7.07)}=0.99999$: this is the *probability that* $Z<z$; since we are interested in the probability that $Z>z$, we need to consider the complementary value $p=1-F(z)=0.00001$.

We have $p<p^*$, where $p^*=0.05=5\%$ is the *desired significance level*.

>[!note] NOTE
>This approach is perhaps more in line with the explanation of hypothesis testing given at the beginning of the section.
>The intuition behind it is that **if H0 were true, the probability of observing a result at least as extreme** [as the one we're investigating] would be so low that it's **reasonable to reject the null hypothesis** and thus accept our own.
>Note that *the higher the confidence level*, the *smaller our p value* needs to be to reject H0.
## T TEST
The **t-test** is *very similar to the z-test*, with the key difference that the t-test can be used **when the population variance is not known**.
The *test statistic* is given by:
$$
	t = \frac{\bar{X}-\mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{ n }}
$$

>[!warning] ATTENTION
>In this case, $\sigma$ is the **sample standard deviation**, while in the z-test it was the *population standard deviation*.

This test is based on **Student's t distribution**, which is *bell-shaped* and *symmetric*, just like the normal distribution, but has **fatter tails**.
This is to account the fact that we're considering the **sample standard deviation instead of the true population standard deviation**.

As a result, *t distribution tables* have *different values* from those found in a *z table*:

![[03_01_tdistribution_table.png]]

>[!note] DEGREES OF FREEDOM
>Note that the table is organised in rows depending on a parameter called **df**: these are the **degrees of freedom of the distribution**.
>In our case, we simply have $\text{df}=n-1$, but interested readers can find more details in > [[#DETAILS ON DOF]].

To better understand how this test works, let's walk through an example.

>[!tldr] EXAMPLE
>Suppose the **hypothesised mean score** of students is **12**.
>Suppose we take a sample of students and their marks are:
>$$ \text{10.5, 9, 7, 12, 8.5, 7.5, 6.5, 8, 11, 9.5}  $$
>Is there enough evidence to support that *the two groups* (the sample and the overall student population) have **different means** with a 5% significance level?

We thus have:
- **H0**: the **true difference between the groups is zero**.
- **H1**: the **true difference is different from zero**.

>[!warning] ATTENTION
>We are conducting a **two tailed test** this time: the reason is that we *choose the test* **before conducting the experiment**. 
>Since the students are selected *after choosing to use a particular test* and we *don't yet know what their average score is*, we **can only assume the two means differ**, but we *can't yet tell in "which direction" they differ*.
>
>**NOTE**: Since the test *must be chosen before*, most analyses require a **two tailed test**. We *only use one tailed tests* when we *already have some evidence* (or some kind of information) about the "direction" of the inequality.

The first step is to compute the sample mean and standard deviation.
We obtain $\mu=8.95$ and $\sigma=1.8$. Since $n=10$ we have $\text{df}=9$.

Let's now compute the test statistic:
$$
	t = \frac{8.95-12}{1.80 / \sqrt{ 10 }} \approx -5.352
$$
With a 5% significance level, a two-tailed test and $9$ degrees of freedom, we obtain (from the table) the following critical value $t^*$:
$$
	t^* = 2.262
$$
Since we have:
$$
	|-5.352| > 2.262
$$
We can **reject the null hypothesis** and say that, indeed, **there is a difference between the sample mean and population mean**.
Note that we could use the absolute value because of the symmetry of the distribution.
## CONFIDENCE INTERVALS
We can use **normal distribution** and **Student distribution tables** to **estimate how accurately the sample mean estimates the population mean**.

This is often expressed as a **confidence interval**.
The confidence interval (*CI*) at a given *confidence level* is calculated as follows:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\bar{X} &\pm z \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{ n }} \\ \\
		\bar{X} &\pm t \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{ n }}
	\end{align*}
$$

>[!note] NOTE
>The first case refers to *normal distributions*, the second to *Student t distributions*.
>In any case, $z$ and $t$ are the values associated with the desired *confidence level*.

Let's take a look at an example:

![[03_02_confidence_interval.png]]

Assume we have gathered the data in the table above.
We want to generate a **95% confidence interval for systolic blood pressure**.

The $z$ value for 95% is $z=1.96$. Thus, we obtain:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		127.3 &\pm 1.96 \frac{19}{\sqrt{ 3534 }} \\ \\
		127.3 &\pm 0.63
	\end{align*}
$$
Therefore the **95% confidence interval for systolic blood pressure** is:
$$
	(126.7,127.9)
$$

>[!warning] MEANING
>We are **95% confident the true mean** systolic blood pressure is **in the interval above**.
>This **does not mean** that **there is a 95% probability** that the **true mean is in the interval**; it means that **if we can keep repeating the observations** over and over, we will **observe a mean inside that interval 95% of the times**. 
# CORRELATION
Correlation is a **statistical measure** that determines **how closely two variables fluctuate**. 
- A *positive correlation* shows the extent to which those *variables increase or decrease in parallel*. 
- A *negative correlation* shows the extent to which *one variable increases as the other decreases*.
- A *low correlation* value shows that *the two variables* are *weakly influenced by one another*.

There **isn't one universal way to compute correlation**; in fact, there are many different approaches, each with their *pros and cons*.

Here, we present just two definitions of correlation: **Spearman's** and **Pearson's**.
## PEARSON CORRELATION
Pearson correlation measures the **strength of the linear relationship between two random variables**.

>[!def] PEARSON CORRELATION
>Consider two random variables $X$ and $Y$ with standard deviations $\sigma_{X}$ and $\sigma_{Y}$. Pearson's correlation coefficient is defined as follows:
>$$
>	\rho = \frac{ \text{cov}(X,Y) }{ \sigma_{X}\sigma_{Y} }
>$$

Note that it is essentially a **normalised measurement of the covariance**, such that the result always has a value between $-1$ and $+1$.
In particular, the **sign** of $\rho$ tells us the **direction of the relationship** between the two variables, while the **absolute value** of $\rho$ tells us **its strength**:
- $0.0<|\rho|<0.1$ - *no* correlation.
- $0.1<|\rho|<0.3$ - *low* correlation.
- $0.3<|\rho|<0.5$ - *medium* correlation.
- $0.5<|\rho|<0.7$ - *high* correlation.
- $0.7<|\rho|<1.0$ - *very high* correlation.

![[03_03_Pearson_correlation_explanation.png]]

>[!idea] OBSERVATION
>A **low Pearson correlation** value does **not necessarily mean** the two **variables aren't correlated**.
>As stated above, $\rho$ measure the **strength** of the **linear relationship** between the two: if the variables are correlated, but their **relationship is non-linear**, then Pearson's coefficient **can't detect it**.

For discrete random variables (which cover most of the applications we're interested in), we can rewrite $\rho$ in terms of the *observed values* $x_{i}$ and $y_{i}$ and the *mean values* $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$:
$$
	\rho = \frac{\sum_{i}(x_{i}-\bar{x})(y_{i}-\bar{y})}{ \sqrt{ \sum_{i}(x_{i}-\bar{x})^2 } \sqrt{ \sum_{i}(y_{i}-\bar{y})^2 } }
$$

Let's now look at an example. Consider the scores of 7 students in *maths* and *science*:

| STUDENT | MATHS ($X$) | SCIENCE ($Y$) |
| ------- | ----------- | ------------- |
| 1       | 98          | 88            |
| 2       | 87          | 92            |
| 3       | 91          | 95            |
| 4       | 75          | 82            |
| 5       | 81          | 74            |
| 6       | 68          | 78            |
| 7       | 93          | 96            |
We have $\bar{x}=84.71$ and $\bar{y}=86.43$.

We now compute the values to find $\rho$:

| STUDENT | $x_{i}-\bar{x}$ | $y_{i}-\bar{y}$ | $(x_{i}-\bar{x})(y_{i}-\bar{y})$ | $(x_{i}-\bar{x})^2$ | $(y_{i}-\bar{y})^2$ |
| ------- | --------------- | --------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------- |
| 1       | 13.29           | 1.57            | 20.87                            | 176.62              | 2.46                |
| 2       | 2.29            | 5.57            | 12.76                            | 5.24                | 31.02               |
| 3       | 6.29            | 8.57            | 53.91                            | 39.56               | 73.44               |
| 4       | -9.71           | -4.43           | 43.02                            | 94.28               | 19.62               |
| 5       | -3.71           | -12.43          | 46.12                            | 13.76               | 154.5               |
| 6       | -16.71          | -8.43           | 140.87                           | 279.22              | 71.06               |
| 7       | 8.29            | 9.57            | 79.34                            | 68.72               | 91.58               |
And thus we find:
$$
	\rho = 0.724
$$
The performances in mathematics and science are **positively (highly) correlated**.

>[!note] REPORTING THE CORRELATION VALUE
>When we **report a correlation value** we also need to accompany it with information regarding the **statistical significance** of our observation.

The general form of this report consists in **stating the null hypothesis** and **rejecting it with some statistical significance**.
Usually, **H0** is stated as follows:
>H0: There is **no linear association between the variables** in the population.

We can *decide whether to reject H0 or not* by using a **t test**, which in the case of Pearson's correlation is given by:
$$
	t = \rho\frac{ \sqrt{ n-2 }}{\sqrt{ 1-\rho^2 }}
$$
>[!note] NOTE
>1. A $p$-value can be obtained from a **t distribution table** with $\text{df}=n-2$. Intuitively, we're *losing two degrees of freedom* because we are *estimating two values* (the *mean for each variable*) before calculating $\rho$.
>2. By rewriting the above formula in the following way, we immediately recognise it has the **same form as the t statistic** seen in the relevant section:
>   $$ t = \frac{\rho - 0}{\sqrt{ 1-\rho^2 } / \sqrt{ n-2 }} $$
>   Where: 
>   - $r=0$ is the **value of the correlation according to H0** (which claims *there is no correlation*). 
>   - $\sigma=\sqrt{ 1-\rho^2 }$ is the **fraction of the standard deviation** that is **not explained by the correlation**.

>[!warning] CLARIFICATION
>It is important to realise that **statistical significance does not indicate the strength of Pearson's correlation**. 
>In fact, achieving a value of $p=0.001$, for example, does *not mean that the relationship is stronger* than if we had achieved a value of $p=0.04$. 
>This is because the significance test is **only investigating whether we can reject or fail to reject the null hypothesis**.
## SPEARMAN CORRELATION
We've seen how Pearson's correlation is *limited to linear relationships*. 
Spearman's correlation, also known as **Spearman's rank coefficient**, *overcomes this limitation* by evaluating **how well** the correlation between two variables **can be formulated by a monotonic function**.

To achieve this, Spearman's correlation uses **data rank** to **measure monotonicity** between **ordinal or continuous variables**.

>[!def] SPEARMAN'S RANK COEFFICIENT
>Consider two random variables $X$ and $Y$, with standard deviations $\sigma_{X}$ and $\sigma_{Y}$. 
>Spearman's rank coefficient is defined as the **Pearson correlation between the rank variables**:
>$$ r_{s} = \rho[R[X],R[Y]] = \frac{\text{cov}[R[X], R[Y]]}{\sigma_{R[X]} \sigma_{R[Y]}} $$
>Where $d_{i}:=R[X_{i}]-R[Y_{i}]$ is the *difference between the two ranks of each observation*.

>[!note] SIMPLIFICATION
>Only when all $n$ **ranks are distinct integers** (there are no ties), the coefficient can be computed using the popular (simpler) formula:
>$$ r_{s} = 1 - \frac{6\Sigma d_{i}^2}{n(n^2-1)} $$

Note that $r_{s}$ can take values in the range $[-1,+1]$ where:
- A value of **+1** means there is a *perfect association of rank*. This means the *correlation* of the two variables can be *perfectly described by a monotonic (increasing) function*.
- A value of **0** means *there is no association between ranks*.
- A value of **-1** means *perfect negative association of rank*. This means the *correlation* of the two variables can be *perfectly described by a monotonic (decreasing) function*.

Once again, let's look at example to better understand how to calculate $r_{s}$.
Consider the scores of 5 students in maths and science, as reported by the table below:

| STUDENT | MATHS ($X$) | SCIENCE ($Y$) |
| ------- | ----------- | ------------- |
| A       | 35          | 24            |
| B       | 20          | 35            |
| C       | 49          | 39            |
| D       | 44          | 48            |
| E       | 30          | 45            |
We need to *compute the rank of each observation*: the highest mark will get a rank of 1, the lowest will get 0:

| STUDENT | MATHS ($X$) | $R[x_{i}]$ | SCIENCE ($Y$) | $R[y_{i}]$ | $d_{i}$ | $d_{i}^2$ |
| ------- | ----------- | ---------- | ------------- | ---------- | ------- | --------- |
| A       | 35          | 3          | 24            | 5          | 2       | 4         |
| B       | 20          | 5          | 35            | 4          | 1       | 1         |
| C       | 49          | 1          | 39            | 3          | 2       | 4         |
| D       | 44          | 2          | 48            | 1          | 1       | 1         |
| E       | 30          | 4          | 45            | 2          | 2       | 4         |
Notice that there are *no tied scores*, which means we can use the simplified version of the formula.
We thus obtain:
$$
	r_{s} = 1 - \frac{6\cdot14}{5(25-1)} = 0.3
$$
Since the value isn't particularly high, we conclude that there's a **weak correlation** between the two ranks.

Just like before, we also need to provide information regarding the **statistical significance of our observation**.
To do so, we need to *set the desired significance level* and then *check whether our computed Spearman correlation coefficient is high enough*.
To check this, we use the **Spearman critical value table** below:

![[03_04_Spearman_critical_table.png]]

>[!note] NOTE
>1. To get the critical value we need to check the **row corresponding** to $n-2$.
>2. The **nominal alpha** is referred to a **one tailed test**. If we're performing a *two tailed test* we need to consider $\alpha/2$.

Note that, *in most cases*, we **use a two-tailed test** for correlation. 
This is because we are simply asking: *is there a relationship between these two variables?* We **don't mind if the correlation is positive or negative**. 
However, we use a **one-tailed test when we have prior information** (or a specific *prediction* or *theory*) about the **direction of the relationship**. 
For example, we could predict that more hours of study will increase exam scores. In this case, we are only looking for a positive correlation; we do not care about a negative one.
## PEARSON AND SPEARMAN COMPARISON
**Pearson** correlation is particularly good at **capturing linear relationships** between variables. 
Its sensitivity to linear patterns makes it a powerful tool when investigating relationships governed by a *consistent linear trend*. Moreover, the standardised nature of the coefficient allows for easy comparison across different datasets. 

However, it's crucial to note that Pearson is **susceptible to the influence of outliers**. 
If a dataset contains **extreme values** they **can impact the calculation**, leading to **inaccurate interpretations**. 
Pearson correlation **effectiveness diminishes when faced with curvilinear patterns**. 
This limitation arises from Pearson's **inherent assumption of linearity**, making it ill-suited to capture the nuances of non-linear relationships. 

Unlike Pearson, **Spearman does not assume linearity** and is **robust to outliers**. 
It focuses on the ordinal nature of data, making it a valuable tool when the relationship between variables is **more about the order than the specific values**. 
In fact, Spearman **assesses monotonicity**, which can be linear, but also non-linear.

>[!note] OTHER APPROACHES TO CORRELATION
>Notice that there are **other correlation approaches** proposed in the literature, such as **Kendall Tau** (or simply *Kendall’s correlation*), which is a *non parametric alternative* to Pearson’s correlation. 
>Since it also identifies monotonic relationships between the variables, it is **commonly considered as an alternative to Spearman’s correlation**. 
>Spearman is preferred for *weak correlations in small datasets*; in **large samples, Kendall Tau's reliability surpasses Spearman**. 
>Kendall’s Tau is a robust estimator against outliers and *non-normality* (non-normal distributions).
## DETAILS ON DOF
**Estimates** of statistical parameters can be **based upon different amounts of information** about the data. 

>[!def] DEGREES OF FREEDOM (STATISTICS)
>Degrees of freedom are the **number of independent pieces of information** that go into the **estimate of a parameter**.

In general, the degrees of freedom of an estimate of a parameter are equal to the **number of independent observations** that go into the estimate **minus the number of parameters used as intermediate steps** in the estimation of the parameter itself. 

For example, if the variance is to be estimated from a random sample of $n$ independent scores, then the degrees of freedom are $n-1$ (number of independent scores, $n$, minus the number of parameters estimated as intermediate steps, in this case, the variance). 

When computing the **Pearson's correlation**, we are **estimating two parameters**: the *mean for each variable*.

>[!idea] OBSERVATION
>After all, we said that Pearson's correlation measures the *strength of a linear relationship between the variables*.
>Now, *how many parameters do we need to completely characterise a linear relationship?* The answer is **two**: a **slope** and an **intercept**.
>In fact, computing Pearson's correlation is *equivalent* to fitting the model:
>$$ Y = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}X $$
>We **start with n data points**, then **two parameters are estimated**, and thus the residual degrees of freedom are $n-2$.

Mathematically, degrees of freedom can also be interpreted as the **number of dimensions of the domain of a random vector**, or essentially the *number of "free" components* (how many components need to be known before the vector is fully determined).

To better understand this interpretation, consider the following process:
- Let's assume we made $n$ *independent measurements*. We can represent this *set of observations* with a *(random) vector* $v=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in \mathbb{R}^n$, where **each component** $x_{i}$ is the **i-th observation**. 
  At this point, since we have **no constraints, all n components can vary freely**.
- We then **compute the mean** of the sample. This means **adding a constraint** of the form $x_{1}+x_{2}+\dots+x_{n}=n\bar{x}$: an *equation that* **forces one of the components**.
  The **random vector** can now **only vary in a subspace of the original**; since there is just one constraint, we're effectively forcing the vector into $\mathbb{R}^{n-1}$.
- Similarly, every time we *estimate a parameter* we are **adding a constraint** that **further reduces the dimension of the subspace** the random vector is *forced to be in*. 

Now let's see how this interpretation works in the case of **correlation**.
Now, **each observation is a pair of values** $(x_{i},y_{i})$, and since we made $n$ observations, we have $2n$ **total values**.

This means that the vector representing the observations has $2n$ components. Furthermore, we know that to calculate the correlation between $X$ and $Y$ we **need to estimate 2 parameters**.
Thus, we are lead to think that the *leftover* **degrees of freedom** should be $2n-2$; but wait, we have seen that, in reality, the degrees of freedom are $n-2$. 
How do we explain this discrepancy?

>[!idea] KEY INTUITION
>The answer lies in the fact that **assuming the two variables are correlated** translates to constraints of the form:
>$$ Y = f(X) \implies y_{i} = f(x_{i}) \text{ , }i=1,2,\dots,n $$

This means we **actually have n+2 constraints**: $n$ coming from the *assumption of correlation* and 2 being the *estimated means*.
Thus, we finally obtain $\text{df}=2n-(n+2)=n-2$.
# SURVIVAL ANALYSIS
We are often interested in *assessing whether there are differences in survival* (or cumulative incidence of event) *among different groups of participants in a statistical study*. For example, in a clinical trial with a survival outcome, we might be interested in *comparing survival between participants* receiving the *drug chosen by the  neural network A* as compared to the *drug chosen by the neural network B*.

>[!def] SURVIVAL ANALYSIS
>Survival analysis is a branch of statistics for **analysing the expected duration of time until one event occurs**, such as death in biological organisms or failure in mechanical systems.
>This topic is often called **reliability theory**, **reliability analysis** engineering.

In this section, topics will be presented from the perspective of the *analysis of biological systems* for the sake of intuition, but the same concepts can be applied to many other fields just by adopting a **different definition** of **"experiencing an event"** , as seen in the definition above.

Before diving in, we present the *context* of this kind of studies and the *problems we're facing* when conducting them.

For a start, **complete data** (actual *time to event data*) **is not always available** on each participant in a study. 
In many studies, *participants are enrolled over a period of time* (months or years) and the study ends on a specific calendar date. Thus, participants who **enrol later are followed for a shorter period** than participants who enrol early. Some participants may also *drop out of the study before the end of the follow-up period* (e.g., move away, become disinterested) and others may *die during the follow-up period* (assuming the outcome of interest is not death).

In each of these instances, we have **incomplete follow-up information**. 
True survival time (sometimes called failure time) is not known because the study ends or because a participant drops out of the study before experiencing the event. 
What we know is that the participants' survival time is greater than their last observed follow-up time. These times are called **censored times**.

In general, there are **different types of censoring**. 
The most common is called **right censoring** and occurs when a **participant does not have the event of interest during the study** and thus their last observed follow-up time is less than their time to event. 
This can occur when a *participant drops out of the study before it ends* or when a *participant is event free at the end of the observation period*.

Consider the following example:

>[!tldr] EXAMPLE
>Participants are **recruited** into the study **over a period of two years** and are **followed for up to 10 years**. 
>The figure below indicates *when they enrolled* and *what subsequently happened to them* during the observation period.

![[03_05_survival_intro1.png]]

During the study period, *three participants suffer myocardial infarction* (**MI**), *one dies*, *two drop out* of the study (for unknown reasons), and *four complete the 10-year follow-up* without suffering MI. 
The next figure shows the same data, but survival times are now starting at a common "**zero time**"  (i.e., as if all participants enrolled in the study at the same time).

![[03_06_survival_intro2.png]]

Based on this data, we may be interested in calculating **what is the likelihood that a participant will suffer an MI over 10 years**.
**Three** of the 10 participants **suffer MI** over the course of follow-up, so we are lead to think that an **estimate for the likelihood** is **30%**.

>[!idea] OBSERVATIONS
>1. The problem is that this is **probably an underestimate** of the true likelihood as **two participants dropped out** and might have suffered an MI had they been observed for the full 10 years.  Their **observed times are censored**. 
>2. Furthermore, **one participant dies** after 3 years of follow-up, raising the question of whether we should include it in the analysis or not.
>3. **If we included these** 3 participants, **how should we proceed** with the statistical analysis?

Notice that if we *exclude all three participants who haven't completed the follow up study*, the estimate of the likelihood that a participant suffers an MI is **3/7 = 43%**, **substantially higher than the initial estimate** of 30%. 

The fact that all participants are often not observed over the entire follow-up period makes survival data unique. 
In this small example, participant 4 is observed for 4 years and over that period does not have an MI. Participant 7 is observed for 2 years and over that period does not have an MI. While hey do not suffer the event of interest, they still **contribute important information** that **should be accounted for**. 
Survival analysis techniques are aimed at making use of this information in the estimate of the probability of event.

>[!note] NOTE
>There are a number of **popular parametric methods** that are used to model survival data, and they differ in terms of the **assumptions** that are made about the **distribution of survival times** in the population.
>In this section, we **focus on non parametric methods**, which make **no assumptions about how the probability** that a person experiences the event **changes over time**.

## LOG RANK TEST
The **log rank tes**t is a popular test to *test the null hypothesis of no difference in survival* between *two or more independent groups*.
The test compares the entire survival experience between groups and can be thought of as a test of **whether the survival curves are identical (overlapping) or not**.

Let's discuss a case with 2 curves: $S_{1t}$ and $S_{2t}$, and suppose we want *statistical significance* $\alpha=0.05$.
The hypotheses at play are:
- **H0**: The **two survival curves are identical**.
- **H1**: The two survival curves are **different**.

The log rank statistic is **approximately distributed as a chi-square** test statistic. 
There are *several forms of the test statistic*, and they vary in terms of how they are computed. We use the following:
$$
	\chi^2 = \sum_{j} \frac{\left( \sum_{t} O_{jt} - \sum_{t} E_{jt} \right)^2}{\sum_{t} E_{jt}}
$$
Where $\sum_{t}O_{jt}$ represents the **sum** of the **observed number of events in the j-th group** over time and $\sum_{t}E_{jt}$ represents the **sum** of the **expected number of events in the j-th group** over time.

To see the test in action, let's apply it to the following case of study:

>[!tldr] EXAMPLE
>A small clinical trial is run to **compare two combinations of treatments** in patients with advanced gastric cancer. 
>**Twenty participants** with stage IV gastric cancer who consent to participate in the trial are randomly assigned to be treated with **protocol A** or **protocol B**.

Six participants in the *chemotherapy before surgery* group die over the course of follow-up as compared to three participants in the *chemotherapy after surgery* group. 
Other participants in each group are *followed for varying numbers of months*, some to the end of the study at 48 months (in the chemotherapy after surgery group). 
Using the procedures outlined earlier, we begin applying the **Kaplan-Meier approach**.

![[03_07_LRTest_example1.png]]

The resulting survival curves are shown below:

![[03_08_LRTest_example2.png]]

>[!idea] OBSERVATION
>The survival probabilities for the *chemotherapy after surgery* group are **higher** than the survival probabilities for the *chemotherapy before surgery* group, **suggesting a survival benefit**. 
>However, these survival curves are **estimated from small samples**.

Now we proceed to compute the $\chi^2$ value. To do so, we need the **observed events** and the **expected number of events** at **each time step**; we know the former, but lack the latter.
To obtain these, we need to calculate what the **expected number of events** would be **if the null hypothesis were true**.

>[!idea] EXPECTED NUMBER OF EVENTS
>Since H0 states that *all groups have the same survival experience*, we **expect the events** to be **uniformly distributed in the two groups**. That is:
>$$ E_{1t} = O_{t} \frac{N_{1t}}{N_{t}} \text{ , } E_{2t} = O_{t} \frac{N_{2t}}{N_{t}} $$

Furthermore, the **total number of (observed) events is fixed**. This means that (assuming we're dealing with $k$ groups), once we know **differences** (between observed and expected events) for $k-1$ groups, the **difference for the last group is also already determined**, since they must sum to zero.
As a consequence, the **degrees of freedom** are $k-1$: this is important because the computed $\chi^2$ value must be *compared against a critical value* obtained from a $\chi^2$ distribution table, with values that *depend on the degrees of freedom* of the distribution itself.

We thus obtain the following table:

|     MONTH      | $N_{1t}$ | $N_{2t}$ | TOTAL $N_{t}$ | $O_{1t}$ | $O_{2t}$ | TOTAL $O_{t}$ | $E_{1t}$ | $E_{2t}$ |
| :------------: | :------: | :------: | :-----------: | :------: | :------: | :-----------: | :------: | :------: |
|       8        |    10    |    10    |      20       |    1     |    0     |       1       |  0.500   |  0.500   |
|       12       |    8     |    10    |      18       |    1     |    0     |       1       |  0.444   |  0.556   |
|       14       |    7     |    10    |      17       |    1     |    0     |       1       |  0.412   |  0.588   |
|       21       |    5     |    10    |      15       |    1     |    0     |       1       |  0.333   |  0.667   |
|       26       |    4     |    8     |      12       |    1     |    0     |       1       |  0.333   |  0.667   |
|       27       |    3     |    8     |      11       |    1     |    0     |       1       |  0.273   |  0.727   |
|       28       |    2     |    8     |      10       |    0     |    1     |       1       |  0.200   |  0.800   |
|       33       |    1     |    7     |       8       |    0     |    1     |       1       |  0.125   |  0.875   |
|       41       |    0     |    5     |       5       |    0     |    1     |       1       |  0.000   |  1.000   |
| **ROW TOTAL:** |          |          |               |    6     |    3     |               |  2.620   |  6.380   |
We can finally **calculate the test statistic**:
$$
	\chi^2 = \frac{(6-2.620)^2}{2.620} + \frac{(3-6.380)^2}{6.380} \approx 6.151
$$
Notice that in this example we have $k=2$, therefore $k-1=1$ degree of freedom.
By looking at a *critical value table* for the distribution (these can be found online or in textbooks) we obtain $\chi^2_{*}=3.84$.
Since **we observe** $\chi^2=6.151>\chi^2_{*}=3.84$ we **can reject the null hypothesis**: we have **significant evidence to show that the two curves are different**.

>[!note] NOTE
>Notice that it is a **goodness-of-fit test**: it compares observed data with the expected data and *determines if the difference is due to chance*.
## HAZARD AND HAZARD RATIO
In the context of survival analysis, an important indicator that needs to be reported is the **hazard** $H$ of the group.

>[!def] HAZARD
>It represents the **expected number of events per one unit of time**.

It's a very intuitive metric: if the hazard of a group at time $t$ is $0.2$ and $t$ is measured in months, it means that *on average*, $0.2$ events are **expected per person per month**.
Another interpretation is based on the *reciprocal of the hazard*. In the example above we have $\frac{1}{H}=\frac{1}{0.2}=5$: this means the **expected event-free time** is 5 months per person at risk.

In most situations, we are interested in **comparing groups with respect to their hazards**. 
The hazard ratio is the ratio of the total number of observed to expected events in two independent comparison groups:

>[!def] HAZARD RATE
>Given *two groups* $A$ and $B$, their **hazard ratio** is defined as:
>$$ \text{HR} = \frac{ \sum_{t} O_{At} / \sum_{t} E_{At} }{ \sum_{t} O_{Bt} / \sum_{t} E_{Bt} } $$
>Where $O_{Gt}$ is the number of *observed events in group G at time* $t$ and $E_{Gt}$ is the *number of expected events in group G at time* $t$.

We can calculate the *hazard ratio* from the previous example:
$$
	\text{HR} = \frac{ \frac{O_{t}}{E_{t}}\bigg|_{\text{Chemo before}} }{ \frac{O_{t}}{E_{t}}\bigg|_{\text{Chemo after}} } = 4.870
$$
Thus, the **risk of death** is $4.87$ times **higher** in the chemotherapy before surgery group as compared to the chemotherapy after surgery group.
# COMPARING CLASSIFIERS
An important application of hypothesis testing in machine learning is **comparing the performances of classifiers**.
The goal is to determine whether **different classifiers exhibit different performances** and, in such case, **how large are these differences**.
In the following sections, we present some of the methods used to *compare performances* of classifiers and, in general, other machine learning models. 
## MCNEMAR'S TEST STATISTIC
McNemar's Test (sometimes also called "*within-subjects chi-squared test*", because the test statistic follows a $\chi^2$ distribution) is a **statistical test for paired nominal data**. 
In the context of machine learning (or statistical) models, we can use McNemar's test to **compare the predictive accuracy of two models**.

Given two models, we consider the following table:

|                     | MODEL 2 CORRECT | MODEL 2 WRONG |
| ------------------- | :-------------: | :-----------: |
| **MODEL 1 CORRECT** |       $a$       |      $b$      |
| **MODEL 1 WRONG**   |       $c$       |      $d$      |
Given the table above, the **hypotheses** at play are formulated as follows:
- **H0**: the probabilities $p(b)$ and $p(c)$ are **the same**: none of the two models performs better than the other.
- **H1**: the **two models perform differently**, thus $p(b)\ne p(c)$.

The test statistic follows a **chi squared distribution** with **one degree of freedom** and is computed as follows:
$$
	\chi^2 = \frac{(|b-c|-1)^2}{b+c}
$$
Consider the following example:

|                     | MODEL 2 CORRECT | MODEL 2 WRONG |
| ------------------- | :-------------: | :-----------: |
| **MODEL 1 CORRECT** |      9945       |      15       |
| **MODEL 1 WRONG**   |       25        |      15       |
Can we state that the **two models perform differently**, with $\alpha=0.05$ statistical significance?
By computing the test statistic we obtain $\chi^2=2.025$, which corresponds to a $p$-value of $0.15$. Since this is *larger than the significance threshold*, we **cannot reject the null hypothesis**: there isn't enough evidence to suggest that the two models have different performances.

>[!note] NOTE
>An **exact binomial test** is **recommended for small sample sizes** ($b+c<25$), since the *test statistic* may *not be well-approximated* by the chi-squared distribution. 
>The standard McNemar test assumes continuous data, but in the case of small samples, the possible values of the statistical test are **few and "jump"** from one number to another, thus making the approximation *too optimistic*. 
>The exact $p$-value can be computed as follows:
>$$ p = 2 \sum_{i=b}^n \binom{n}{i} 0.5^i (1-0.5)^{n-i} = 2(0.5^n)\sum_{i=b}^n \binom{n}{i} $$
>Where $n=b+c$.
## WILCOXON SIGNED RANK TEST
The **Wilcoxon signed-ranks test** is a **non-parametric test** which ranks the **differences in performances of two classifiers** for each data set. 

Let's see how it works.
Let $d_{i}$ be the **difference between the performance scores** of the two classifiers on the $i$-th dataset out of the $N$ total.
These differences are **ranked according to their absolute values**, with *average ranks* being assigned *in case of ties*.
Now let $R^+$ be the sum of ranks for the data sets on which the **second model outperformed the first**, and, similarly, let $R^-$ be the sum of ranks for the data sets on which *the first model outperformed the first*:
$$
	\begin{align*}
	R^+ &= \sum_{d_{i}>0} \text{rank}(d_{i}) + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{d_{i}=0} \text{rank}(d_{i}) \\ \\
	R^- &= \sum_{d_{i}<0} \text{rank}(d_{i}) + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{d_{i}=0} \text{rank}(d_{i})
	\end{align*}
$$

>[!idea] OBSERVATION
>Notice that ranks of $d_{i}=0$ are *split evenly across the two* and if there's an odd number of them, one is ignored

Now let $T$ be the **smaller of the two sums**:
$$
	T := \text{min}\{ R^+,R^- \}
$$
This value is then compared to a **critical value** $T^*$, obtained as explained below, to determine whether we can reject or not the null hypothesis.
Notice that, just like earlier, the hypotheses at play are:
- **H0**: the two classifiers have **identical performances**.
- **H1**: the two classifiers have **different performances**.

Most books include a **table of critical values** for $T$ for $N$ up to $25$ (see table in the example below).
Otherwise, for larger numbers of data sets, we can compute a **test statistic** as follows:

>[!def] WILCOXON TEST STATISTIC
>$$ z = \frac{T - \frac{1}{4}N(N+1)}{\sqrt{ \frac{1}{24}N(N+1)(2N+1) }} $$

This test statistic is *approximately normally distributed* and we can thus use $z$-value tables.

Let's take a look at an example.

>[!tldr] EXAMPLE
>Two different **classifiers** (C4.5 and C4.5+m) are used to make *medical predictions* on patients with *prior conditions*.
>The performance of the two classifiers are reported in the following table, along with the ranks of each difference.

![[03_09_Wilcoxon_example.png]]

>[!idea] OBSERVATIONS
>Notice that, as stated above, **average ranks are assigned in case of ties**.
>Here, for example, a difference of $0.000$ appears in 2 data sets: instead of assigning ranks 1 and 2, we assign their average (1.5).

From the table above, we compute:
- $R^+=3.5+9+12+5+6+14+11+13+8+10+1.5=93$
- $R^-=7+3.5++1.5=12$

Therefore we have $T=R^-=12$.

![[03_10_Wilcoxon_table.png]]

According to the **table of critical values** for the Wilcoxon's test, for a confidence level of $\alpha=0.05$ and $N=14$, the **difference between the two classifiers** is significant if $T$ is $\leq 21$: since $T=12<21$ we can **reject H0**.
## COMPARING MULTIPLE CLASSIFIERS
When dealing with the **comparison of multiple classifiers**, a common example of **questionable procedure** is conducting **all possible paired tests** and reporting results in the form of "model A was found to perform significantly better than B and C, model E performed better than D" and so on.
When so many tests are made, a certain "proportion" of the null hypotheses is rejected due to **random chance**, so **listing them makes little sense**.

Instead, we use the following approach.
Let $r_{i}^j$ be the *rank* of the $j$-th of $k$ models on the $i$-th of $N$ *data sets*.
The **Friedman test** compares the **average ranks of models**:
$$
	R_{j} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r_{i}^j
$$
The **null hypothesis** states that **all models are equivalent**.
Thus, if H0 were true, all the ranks $R_{j}$ should be equal and the **Friedman statistic** $\chi^2_{F}$ is distributed according to $\chi^2$ with $k-1$ *degrees of freedom*:

>[!def] FRIEDMAN STATISTIC
>$$ \chi^2_{F} = \frac{12N}{k(k+1)} \left[ \sum_{j=1}^kR_{j}^2 - \frac{k(k+1)^2}{4} \right] $$

Notice that $\chi^2_{F}\sim \chi^2$ when $N$ and $k$ are *large enough* (as a rule of thumb, $N>10$ and $k>5$).
If **number of models and data sets is smaller**, **exact critical values have been computed**.

In any case, Iman and Davenport showed (in 1980) that Friedman's statistic is **undesirably conservative** and derived a better one:
$$
	F_{F} = \frac{(N-1)\chi_{F}^2}{N(k-1) - \chi_{F}^2}
$$
which is distributed according to an **F distribution** with $k-1$ and $(k-1)(N-1)$ **degrees of freedom**. Tables with relevant critical values can be found online or in textbooks.

To better understand this test, let's take a look at an extended version of the example seen in the Wilcoxon's test explanation:

![[03_11_Multiple_classifiers.png]]

We have:
$$
	\chi^2_{F} = \frac{12\cdot14}{4\cdot5} \left[  (3.143^2 + 2.000^2 + 2.893^2 + 1.964^2) - \frac{4\cdot5^2}{4}  \right] = 9.28
$$
And thus:
$$
	F_{F} = \frac{13\cdot9.28}{14\cdot3-9.28} = 3.69
$$
Looking at an F distribution table with a *significance level* $\alpha=0.05$ we find that the **critical value** for $F(3,39)$ is $F^*=2.85$.
Since we have $F_{F}>F^*$ we **can reject the null hypothesis**: we have *significant evidence suggesting the classifiers have different performances*.
## POST-HOC TESTS
>[!warning] CLARIFICATION
>The test discussed above is used to determine **whether the classifiers perform differently** or not, but it gives **no further information**: it doesn't tell us *which* classifier has the best performance, and neither *how much better* the performance is.

As a result, **after rejecting the null hypothesis**, we need to proceed with **post-hoc tests**.
One such example is given by the **Nemenyi test**.

>[!def] NEMENYI TEST
>Given a *pool* of $k$ classifiers, the **performances of two of them are significantly different** if the *corresponding average ranks* **differ by at least the critical difference** $\text{CD}$:
>$$ \text{CD} = q_{\alpha} \sqrt{ \frac{k(k+1)}{6N} } $$
>Where $q_{\alpha}$ are tabulated values (see table below) that depend on the *required significance level* and $N$ is the *number of data sets*.

|     #      |   2   |   3   |   4   |   5   |   6   |   7   |   8   |   9   |  10   |
| :--------: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| $q_{0.05}$ | 1.960 | 2.343 | 2.569 | 2.728 | 2.850 | 2.949 | 3.031 | 3.102 | 3.164 |
| $q_{0.10}$ | 1.645 | 2.052 | 2.291 | 2.459 | 2.589 | 2.693 | 2.780 | 2.855 | 2.920 |

Notice that this test works for comparing *two classifiers from a larger pool*: if we're interested in assessing the performances of *all of the classifiers*, as already stated above, reporting the **results of each pairwise comparison** is **not an acceptable method**.

In this case, it's better to **compare all classifiers** with a **"control" classifier**.
Approaches of this kind are aimed at *controlling the family-wise error* in multiple hypothesis testing by making **corrections** that **adjust critical values** to account for the fact that, while using a *control classifier*, we are **making less comparisons** ($k-1$ instead of $\frac{k(k-1)}{2}$).

>[!def] BONFERRONI-DUNN CORRECTION
>This correction *controls the family-wise error* by **dividing** $\alpha$ by the **number of comparisons made** ($k-1$ in our case).

The corrected critical values are reported in the table below:

|     #      |   2   |   3   |   4   |   5   |   6   |   7   |   8   |   9   |  10   |
| :--------: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| $q_{0.05}$ | 1.960 | 2.241 | 2.394 | 2.498 | 2.576 | 2.638 | 2.690 | 2.724 | 2.773 |
| $q_{0.10}$ | 1.645 | 1.960 | 2.128 | 2.241 | 2.326 | 2.394 | 2.450 | 2.498 | 2.539 |
>[!idea] OBSERVATION
>The **comparison** between the tables for **Nemenyi's** and **Bonferroni-Dunn's** tests reveal that the **power of the post-hoc test** is **much greater** when all classifiers are **compared only to a control classifier** instead of between themselves.
>In fact, values in the second table are lower: this means that *a smaller value is sufficient to obtain the desired* $p$-value.

To better understand these tests, let's apply them to the 4 classifiers of the previous example.
Suppose we want $\alpha=0.05$ significance. 
According to the **Nemenyi** test table, we have $q_{0.05}=2.569$. Thus, the critical difference is:
$$
	\text{CD}_{\text{Nem}} = 2.569 \sqrt{ \frac{4\cdot5}{6\cdot14} } = 1.25
$$
In the example, the **best** and **worst performing** classifiers achieved average ranks of $1.964$ and $3.143$. 
Since **even this difference is less than the critical difference**, we conclude that this test is **not powerful enough to detect any significant differences** between the classifiers.

If we instead **choose C4.5** as the **control classifier**, we can use the *Bonferroni-Dunn-corrected values*, in which case the critical difference is:
$$
	\text{CD}_{\text{Bonf-Dunn}} = 2.394 \sqrt{ \frac{4\cdot5}{6\cdot14} } = 1.16
$$
With this test, we can conclude that:
- *C4.5+m+cf* performs **significantly better** than *C4.5* ($3.143-1.964=1.179>1.16$).
- *C4.5+cf* does **not perform better** ($3.143-2.893=0.250<1.16$).
- *C4.5+m* is **just below the critical difference** ($3.143-2.000=1.143<1.16$).
# BIAS-VARIANCE TRADEOFF
When we talk about model prediction, it’s important to understand **prediction errors** due to **bias** and **variance**. 

>[!def] BIAS ERROR
>**Bias error** is an error from **erroneous assumptions** in the learning algorithm. 
>High bias can cause an algorithm to *miss the relevant relations between features and target outputs* (**underfitting**). 
>It always leads to **high error on training and test data**.

>[!def] VARIANCE ERROR
>The variance is an error from **sensitivity to small fluctuations** in the training set. 
>High variance can cause an algorithm to **model the random noise in the training data**, rather than the intended outputs (**overfitting**). 
>As a result, models with high variance **perform very well on training** data but exhibit **higher error rates on test** data.

The figure below helps us understand these errors: *blue dots* represent the model's *predictions*, the *red circle* in the middle represents the *target predictions*.

![[03_12_BiasVariance.png]]

>[!idea] EXPLANATION
>- **High bias** is caused by a **flaw in the model**: since the model itself is **biased**, the predictions will **always show some "minimum" error** with respect to the target.
>- **High variance** is a result of **overfitting** the training set: the model **learns the random noise** *as if it was important* and thus shows **excessive variance in the prediction outputs**.

When we talk about **"bias variance trade-off** we refer to the problem of **minimising these errors**: it's **not possible to minimise both simultaneously**, hence the term *trade-off*.
## BIAS VARIANCE DECOMPOSITION
Before presenting *why* we *can't minimise both sources of error simultaneously*, we need to understand *how a model's error can be decomposed*.

Let $Y$ be the variable we are *trying to predict* and let $X$ be a *covariate* variable.
The *training set* $D$ consists of a set of points $x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in X$, each associated with a real valued *label* $y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\in Y$.
That is, $D=\{ (x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n}) \}$.
We assume there is a **relationship** between the two such that:
$$
	Y = f(X) + \varepsilon
$$
Where $\varepsilon$ is **random noise**, distributed with **zero mean** and **variance** $\sigma^2$.
Thus, for each point in the training set we have:
$$
	y_{i} = f(x_{i}) + \varepsilon_{i}
$$
Where $\varepsilon_{i}$ is a *noise sample*.

Our **goal** is to **find a function** $\hat{f}(X)$ that **approximates** $f$ **as well as possible** over all $x \in X$.
To achieve this, we want to **minimise the mean squared error** (*MSE*) between each $y$ and $\hat{f}(x)$:
$$
	\text{Err}(x) = \mathbb{E}[ (y-\hat{f}(x))^2 ]
$$
In particular, this error can be **decomposed as follows**:
$$
	\text{Err}(x) = ( \text{Bias}[\hat{f}(x)] )^2 + \text{Var}[\hat{f}(x)] + \sigma^2
$$
Where:
$$
	\begin{align*}
		\text{Bias}[\hat{f}(x)] &= \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)-f(x)] \\
		&= \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - f(x) \\ \\
		\text{Var}_{D}[\hat{f}(x)] &= \mathbb{E}[ ( \hat{f}(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] )^2 ] \\ \\
		\sigma^2 &= \mathbb{E}[ (Y-f(x))^2 ]
	\end{align*}
$$
Notice that *all three terms are non negative*.

>[!idea] IRREDUCIBLE ERROR
>The term $\sigma^2$ is **independent of our model**: it appears because of the *random noise on the labels* and is thus defined "**irreducible error**".

>[!check] DERIVATION
>The derivation of the bias variance decomposition for squared error proceeds as follows.
>
>We start by explicitly writing the *mean squared error* of our model:
>$$
>\begin{align*}
>	\text{Err}(x) &= \mathbb{E}[ (Y-\hat{f}(x))^2 ] \\ \\
>	&= \mathbb{E}[ (f(x) + \varepsilon - \hat{f}(x) )^2 ] \\ \\
>	&= \mathbb{E}[ (f(x)-\hat{f}(x))^2 ] + 2\mathbb{E}[( f(x) - \hat{f}(x) )\varepsilon] + \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^2]
>\end{align*}
>$$
>
>Since $\varepsilon$ is **independent** from $X$, we notice:
>$$
>	\mathbb{E}[(f(x) - \hat{f}(x) )\varepsilon] = \mathbb{E}[f(x)-\hat{f}(x)]\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0
>$$
>
>Furthermore, since $\varepsilon$ has **mean zero**, it immediately follows that:
>$$ \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^2] = \mathbb{E}[(\varepsilon -0)^2] = \sigma^2$$
>
>Thus, we need only expand the remaining term:
>$$
>	\begin{align*}
>		\mathbb{E}[ (f(x)-\hat{f}(x))^2 ] &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \left( f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] + \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - \hat{f}(x) \right)^2 \right] \\ \\
>		&= \mathbb{E} \left[ \left( f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] \right)^2 \right] + \mathbb{E} \left[ \left( \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - \hat{f}(x) \right)^2 \right] \\ \\
>		&+ 2\mathbb{E} \left[ \left( f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] \right) \left( \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x) - \hat{f}(x)] \right) \right]
>	\end{align*}
>$$
>We now show that:
>$$
>	\begin{align*}
>		\mathbb{E} \left[ (f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)])^2 \right] &= \mathbb{E}[f(x)^2] - 2\mathbb{E} \left[ f(x)\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] \right] + \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)^2] \right] \\ \\
>		&= f(x)^2 -2f(x)\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] + \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]^2 \\ \\
>		&= \left(f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] \right)^2
>	\end{align*}
>$$
>This comes from the fact that $f(X)$ is **not a random variable**, but a **deterministic function** of the values $x \in X$ (and thus, $\mathbb{E}[f(x)]=f(x)$ and $\mathbb{E}[f(x)^2]=f(x)^2$).
>With a similar reasoning, we show that the **last term is null**:
>$$
>	\begin{align*}
>		&\mathbb{E} \left[ \left( f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] \right) \left( \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x) - \hat{f}(x)] \right) \right] \\ \\
>		&= \mathbb{E} \left[ f(x)\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] -f(x)\hat{f}(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]^2 + \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]\hat{f}(x) \right] \\ \\
>		&= f(x)\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - f(x)\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]^2 + \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]^2 \\ \\
>		&= 0
>	\end{align*}
>$$
>Lastly, we notice that the remaining term can be rewritten as follows:
>$$
>	\mathbb{E} \left[ \left( \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - \hat{f}(x) \right)^2 \right] = \mathbb{E} \left[ \left( \hat{f}(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] \right)^2 \right]
>$$
>
>We can finally **plug our derivations back** into the equation and **identify each term**:
>$$
>	\text{Err}(\hat{f}) = \underbrace{ \left( f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] \right)^2 }_{ \displaystyle\text{Bias}[\hat{f}(x)]^2 } + \underbrace{ \mathbb{E} \left[ \left( \hat{f}(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] \right)^2 \right] }_{ \displaystyle\text{Var}[\hat{f}(x)] } + \sigma^2
>$$

>[!idea] OBSERVATION
>Notice that $\text{Err}(x)$ **depends on the training point** $(x,y)\in D$.
>We can compute the **error loss function** by taking the *expectation value over the entire set* $X$ used in the training set $D$:
>$$ \text{Err}(\hat{f}) = \mathbb{E}_{x} \left[ \text{Bias}[\hat{f}(x)]^2 + \text{Var}[\hat{f}(x)] \right] + \sigma^2 $$
## OPTIMAL BALANCE
We've seen how the error can be expressed as the **sum** of the model's **bias squared**, the **variance** and an **irreducible error**.
While the **latter** will *always be present*, no matter *how good our model is*, because its **origin lies in the data itself**, the **first two terms can be optimised**.

![[03_13_BiasVariance.png]]

As seen in the figure above, if our model is **too simple** (e.g. has too few parameters), it may exhibit **high bias** because it *underestimates the complexity of the problem*, and **low variance** because, generally, simpler models produce *more predictable data* with *less variations around the expected output*.

On the other hand, if our model is **excessively complex** (e.g. has a large number of parameters), it’s going to introduce **high variance** and **low bias** because it *perfectly learns the training examples*, even the *noise in the data*, and thus predictions look **perfectly accurate in the training set**, but **vary unpredictably** in the **test set**.

The goal is to **optimise this trade off** by finding the *right balance* in the *model complexity* without overfitting or underfitting the data.
